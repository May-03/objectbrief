> **Quick answer.** There is no single agreed method, so pick the one that avoids the two real risks: friction and compression. One garment brand says to roll soft knits and keep them in a breathable cotton bag, avoiding plastic cubes and compression bags, while a men's lifestyle guide says delicate cashmere should be folded, not rolled, and laid flat across the width of the case. A luggage brand, in turn, recommends a compression cube for sweaters. The reconciliation used here: fold or loosely roll, wrap or bag the knit so it does not rub against zips and structured items, fill the case to about three quarters, do not use compression, and unpack promptly. Creases from a trip are mostly a steaming problem, not a damage problem, and one luggage brand says they fall out when cashmere is hung up afterwards. No packing method here was tested for this guide.

## What actually goes wrong in a suitcase

Hayden Hill, a menswear brand, names two mechanisms. Friction, where garments rub against each other or the lining, wears the yarn, lifts surface fibres and leads to pilling on soft knits. Compression, where heavy items press on knits, flattens the air pockets and leaves creases that need heat or steam to remove [1]. It adds that cashmere and wool rely on a microscopic crimp for elasticity and loft, which is why it warns against deep compression [1].

Antler, a luggage brand, takes a more relaxed view: cashmere will crease in transit, but the creases vanish if you hang the pieces up after the journey [3]. A professional packer quoted by Yahoo says silk, wool, denim and cashmere are less likely to wrinkle than other fabrics [6], and Cabin Zero adds that thicker yarns are less likely to wrinkle than loose-knit ones [7]. These do not contradict Hayden Hill so much as measure different things. Creasing is cosmetic and usually recoverable; pilling and loft loss are wear. A packing method should protect the second even if it accepts some of the first.

## Fold, roll or cube: the sources disagree

|Source|What it advises for knits|Note|
|---|---|---|
|Hayden Hill [1]|Roll soft knits to avoid hard folds; keep in a breathable cotton bag; avoid plastic cubes and synthetic compression bags|Garment brand; cashmere named|
|Bespoke Post [2]|Fold, do not roll; lay flat across the width of the bag; do not cram|Men's lifestyle site; short passage on cashmere|
|Eagle Creek [4]|Compression cube for sweaters and bulky clothes|Luggage brand selling cubes|
|Antler [3]|Tissue between folded items; hang cashmere after travel|Luggage brand|

Each is a seller or media statement, not a test. Hayden Hill and Eagle Creek point in opposite directions on cubes.

The disagreement has a commercial pattern. The luggage brand sells compression cubes, the garment brand sells cotton bags and says to avoid synthetic compression, and neither shows a test comparing the methods. The reading here is that the rule that matters is not fold versus roll but how much pressure and rubbing the knit takes, and that a cube used without the compression zip is a fair compromise only if the cube itself is not a smooth plastic that traps moisture, which is Hayden Hill's concern. That is a judgement, not a sourced finding.

If you do roll, roll loosely; if you fold, fold to the fewest layers. Bespoke Post's flat fold across the width of the bag [2] and Hayden Hill's loose roll [1] both try to avoid sharp edges, which is where creases and pressure marks concentrate. Whichever you choose, do it the same way each trip so you learn what your sweater does.

## Tissue paper, cotton bags and what touches the knit

Several sources recommend tissue paper between layers to reduce friction: an organiser quoted by AOL says to place tissue between layers of delicate fabrics [4], Eagle Creek says the same [4], and CNN Underscored suggests silk paper or fine tissue so delicate items do not rub, pill or transfer colour, with acid-free tissue made for long-term storage as an option [5]. Hayden Hill mentions tissue and cotton buffers only for tailored jackets, not knits [1]. Applying tissue to a cashmere sweater is therefore an extension of general delicate-fabric advice, not a cashmere-specific recommendation.

The garment-brand source is more specific about what should touch the sweater: fine knits together in a breathable cotton bag, apart from structured trousers, tailored jackets and hardware [1]. Zips, buckles, belt fittings and rough shoe edges are the usual snag risk for a soft knit, so the practical rule is to put the cashmere on a layer of its own, away from anything with metal or a rough edge.

## How full should the suitcase be

Hayden Hill says overpacking increases compression and suggests filling the case to about 75 percent so there is a cushion of space [1]. TripIt, a travel app, says extra room gives clothes room to breathe and resist wrinkles [13]. Bespoke Post says not to cram cashmere in [2]. Those three line up, which makes this the most reliable rule in the set: leave the case a quarter empty, or put the cashmere on top where nothing sits on it.

If the sweater is bulky, another option is to wear it. Rinse, a laundry service, suggests packing a heavy sweater and a thick scarf in the carry-on so you can put them on at arrival [8]. That avoids compression entirely, and a sweater you carry or wear rarely needs a packing method at all.

## A packing sequence (a synthesis, untested)

- Start with a clean, completely dry sweater. A damp knit packed in a case invites mildew and a stain packed in will set; deal with stains first, and the pages on makeup and general stains explain how.

- Fold the sweater in half lengthwise, then in thirds, or roll it loosely. Pick one method and keep it consistent between trips.

- Wrap it in a sheet of tissue or put it in a breathable cotton bag. Avoid a plastic bag or a compression bag, as Hayden Hill advises [1].

- Place it flat across the width of the case, on top of the other items, and keep it away from zips, belts and shoes [1][2].

- Close the case without forcing it. If the zip strains, take something out; the 75 percent guideline is the check [1].

- Pack the sweater last and unpack it first.

## On arrival: unpack, air and de-crease

Hayden Hill says to unpack promptly to relieve compression, and to hang knits on broad, contoured hangers in a well-ventilated room [1]. Our hang-a-sweater page explains why a hanger through the shoulders is risky and gives the fold-over method for a rail, so use that rather than hanging the sweater by its shoulders in a hotel wardrobe.

For creases, the sources offer the same gentle route: hang the garment in the bathroom while you shower and let the steam relax minor creases [2][1]. Bespoke Post's general instruction is a hot shower with the door shut for about fifteen minutes [2], and Hayden Hill warns against a travel iron's direct heat on cashmere and says excess steam can strip natural oils and make fibres brittle, so steaming should be moderate [1]. No source found gives a safe steaming duration for cashmere, so keep it short and keep a hand's distance.

If the sweater picked up an odour, airing it overnight is the lowest-risk option; Galaxus, a retailer, says fresh air lets cashmere fibres regenerate and reduces the need to wash [14]. That is a retailer's care claim and not a measurement, but it points the same way as the advice to wash cashmere sparingly.

## Worn sweaters and laundry on the road

Keep worn sweaters apart from clean ones. Hayden Hill suggests a separate travel laundry bag so perspiration and residue do not transfer to clean wool or cashmere, and links residue to attracting clothes moths [1]. If you must wash on the road, the cool-water sink method that The Laundress describes in Mr Porter's guide is the least risky: cool water, never hot, a squirt of pH-neutral wool and cashmere shampoo [9]. Dry flat, and allow time for it to dry before you pack it again; the drying page covers the method.

## What about vacuum bags?

Vacuum bags come up in every packing forum. For storage, the sources split: Simplemost says not to vacuum-seal cashmere because it can lose its structure [10], Sunspel says vacuum packing can stretch garments out of shape [11], and Nobody's Child says airtight storage or vacuum packing is fine for longer-term storage [12]. For a trip, nobody the sources read recommends compressing cashmere for the journey, and Hayden Hill's warning against synthetic compression bags [1] applies directly. Our storage page covers the long-term case.

## Carry-on or checked bag

None of the sources the sources read compares carry-on with checked luggage for cashmere, so this is reasoning rather than evidence. A checked bag is stacked, thrown and sat on by other bags, which is the situation in which Hayden Hill's compression warning matters most [1]. A carry-on stays under your control, can go on top of the overhead stack or under the seat, and lets you take the sweater out on a cold flight. If the trip allows only one bag to be checked, put the cashmere in the middle of the clothes, not at the bottom, and keep the case under the 75 percent guideline [1].

## Cardigans, buttons and embellishments

The sources discuss sweaters, not cardigans, so treat this as a practical extension. Button the cardigan before folding so the front does not gape, fold the sleeves in, and keep any buttons, beads or metal trim facing inward or covered with a layer of tissue, since Hayden Hill lists hardware as something to separate from fine knits [1]. Our hanging page gives the same cardigan fold for a rail, and it works as a travel fold too.

## Packing more than one cashmere piece

Several knits can share a cotton bag, which is how Hayden Hill describes fine knits being kept together [1]. Keep the layers few: a stack of five sweaters puts the bottom one under the weight of the rest, and the reading here of the compression warning is that two or three per bag is a safer limit. That number is a judgement call, not a sourced one. If a sweater is dark and another pale, tissue between them also addresses the colour-rubbing concern CNN Underscored mentions [5].

## A home dry run before an important trip

If you are bringing an expensive piece, pack it once at home with the method you plan to use and the suitcase half full of the rest of your clothes, leave it overnight, then take it out and compare it with a sweater that sat in a drawer. Look for creases that do not relax in an hour, a flattened surface on the folded edges, and any surface fuzz where it touched other items. This has not been run and it is not a standard test, but it is cheap and it tells you whether your method works with your own suitcase.

## Questions to ask before you buy travel accessories

- Is the cotton bag or pouch breathable, and large enough to hold the folded sweater without squeezing it?

- If it is a packing cube, does it compress, and is the fabric breathable?

- Is the tissue acid-free? CNN's suggestion of acid-free tissue made for long-term storage [5] is a reasonable standard.

- Does a travel steamer have an adjustable setting, and can you keep it moving rather than holding it still?

- Is the hanger broad and smooth, with no sharp edges, for hotel use?

## Common mistakes

- Compressing the sweater. Hayden Hill and Bespoke Post both say not to cram it [1][2].

- Packing it damp or stained. Moisture and stains do not improve in a closed case.

- Putting it next to metal. Zips and buckles snag a soft knit.

- Ironing it on the trip. Hayden Hill advises against a travel iron's direct heat on cashmere [1].

- Leaving it packed. Unpack promptly to relieve compression [1].

## FAQ

### Should I fold or roll cashmere for travel?

Sources split: one garment brand says roll soft knits, another guide says fold and lay flat across the case [1][2]. Whichever you choose, avoid pressure.

### Will packing cashmere wrinkle it?

Antler says it will crease but the creases vanish when hung up afterwards [3]; Hayden Hill says deep compression creases may need steam [1].

### Can I use packing cubes for cashmere?

Eagle Creek recommends compression cubes for sweaters [4]; Hayden Hill says avoid plastic cubes and synthetic compression bags [1]. Use a cube without compressing it, or use a cotton bag.

### How do I get creases out of cashmere on a trip?

Hang it in the bathroom with the shower running hot and the door shut for a short time [2], and avoid direct iron heat [1].
