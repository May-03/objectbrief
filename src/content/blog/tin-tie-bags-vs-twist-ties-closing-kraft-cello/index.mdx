> **Quick answer.** A tin tie bag has a metal-cored strip attached to the back panel, so the customer folds the top down and bends the tie around the fold. A twist tie is a separate wire-cored strip you wrap around the neck of a bag you have already filled. Both reclose easily, but neither is sold by makers as a barrier seal: one coffee reviewer on a supplier page says he would not store beans in tin tie bags, while some bag pages call the closure airtight. If shelf life matters, heat-seal the bag first and let the tie be the reclosing device. For cello and treat bags, a 4 to 6 inch paper or plastic twist tie with enough overlap is the usual choice. Check size, liner material and whether the bag can be heat sealed before ordering.

## How a tin tie bag is built

A tin tie bag is a paper bag with a flat tie, usually a thin metal strip inside a paper or plastic cover, attached to the back panel. Webstaurant's description of one white kraft bag says the tie is attached to the back of the bag, and that it lets the bag be resealed [2]. A Pactiv bag page describes a tin tie system that is easy to seal and reseal, and says the bag is also heat sealable, which it ties to extending shelf life [1]. That detail matters: the manufacturer is telling you that the tie and a heat seal are two different jobs.

In use, the customer folds the open top over a couple of times and bends the tie ends around the fold. We describe that from general practice, not from a source, and the number of folds is not specified by any page the sources read. Too few folds is a guess at why a bag might spring open; test it.

The two 1 lb bags differ by about an inch in width and an inch in height, which is the gap between a bag that stands neatly and one that tips. Our arithmetic from the table: the Webstaurant 1 lb pocket has a base of 4.25 by 2.5 inches, about 10.6 square inches, against 8.4 for the narrow Interplas bag at 3.375 by 2.5 inches. The wider base is the more stable of the two when full, though neither supplier says so.

For cookies, brownies and other bulky goods the weight label is the wrong yardstick. Measure the product and pick a bag whose pocket clears it, plus room above to fold. Webstaurant's white 2 lb kraft bag with a window, for example, is listed at 6 by 2.75 by 9.5 inches [2], a shape that suits cookies better than a tall coffee bag does.

## Liners: paper alone is not a barrier

Several of these bags are kraft outside with a plastic inside. Interplas lists kraft with a poly liner for its narrow 1 lb bag and a PLA liner for its 5 lb compostable bag [3], Webstaurant lists a polyethylene liner on its 2 lb bag [2], and Restaurantware says its window tin tie bags use a PET lining to stop grease or oil seeping through [4]. Pactiv describes a duplex construction meant to preserve freshness without detailing the layers [1].

For you the practical question is what is inside the paper. A plain kraft bag with no liner suits dry, non-greasy cookies for same-day sale. A greasy product such as a buttery shortbread can show through unlined paper, and the liner is what the PET claim is about [4]. If you want a compostable bag, ask whether the liner and the tie are covered by the certification, because a PLA-lined bag with a metal strip is a mixed product. Nothing here was research the certification rules for this page.

Two practical notes before you order. First, the tie sits on the back panel, so a printed or labelled front keeps its full face; if you plan a sticker on the front, check that it does not cross the fold line, because paper that is stuck down will not fold cleanly. Second, a window bag shows the product but the supplier pages the sources read do not say whether the window film is compostable or recyclable with the paper, so ask [4]. Neither point is tested here; they follow from how the bag is described.

## Is a tin tie closure airtight? The sources disagree

A Baldor listing for Bag Tek kraft tin tie bags says the built-in tin tie keeps food fresh and locks out moisture with an airtight seal [5]. A customer reviewing a 2 lb kraft bag on Webstaurant, from a coffee company, said the tin tie works well and seems to keep beans reasonably fresh, but that he does not recommend storing coffee in them [2]. Restaurantware says its tin tie bags create a tight seal that locks in flavours [4].

These statements are not measurements, and a tin tie closes a folded paper neck, which is not a welded seam. The reading here: the tin tie is a reclosing convenience and a good transit closure, and shelf life depends on the liner and on whether you heat-seal below the fold. If you sell coffee, one-way valves are a separate topic covered on the valve page. For a hand impulse sealer that can run across a lined paper bag, see the impulse sealer pages.

## Loose twist ties for cello and treat bags

Cello and polypropylene treat bags do not come with a tie, so you add one. Twist ties are described by Interplas as a thin metal wire core wrapped in a strip of plastic or paper, sold pre-cut in 4, 5, 6, 8 and 10 inch lengths, with the advice to leave enough overlap for the tie to fit the bag [6]. Tractor Supply describes the same construction and adds that for food the tie belongs on the outside of the bag [7]. A McMaster-Carr page says paper-covered ties are more flexible than plastic-covered ones and are often used for light-duty food packaging [8].

Interplas also says paper and plastic ties share the same wire and differ only in covering: paper ties are less durable and much less water resistant, but have a homemade look suited to crafts, and plastic ties suit wet conditions [6]. Staples says plastic or vinyl ties are water repellent [9]. The 4 inch size is common: a Walmart seller lists 4 inch plastic-coated ties in a 24,000 count pack, and Uxcell lists a 4.72 inch (12 cm) by 0.16 inch (4 mm) foil tie in a 750 count pack [10].

Our untested rule of thumb for length: a tie must wrap the gathered neck of the bag and still leave a twist. A bag neck of 2 inches around needs at least 4 inches of tie, and a thick fold or a gusseted neck calls for 6 inches. Interplas does not give a sizing formula [6], and this has not been tested, so try a few lengths on your own bags before ordering thousands.

## The wire and food rules we did not settle

Twist tie wire is metal, and patent background sections describe what that means in food settings. One patent on a wireless twist tie says wire ties are used in many food packaging applications but cause arcing in a microwave [11]. Another says metal wire ties on food articles preclude metal detectors, since each package would trigger the detector [12]. Those are statements by inventors arguing for their own non-metal ties, not regulatory findings. For a hand-packing small maker with no metal detector the metal-detection point mostly does not apply, but the microwave point suggests a line on your label telling customers to remove the tie before heating, which is a suggestion and not a rule the sources reviewed show.

Interplas's twist tie page names bread bags and cellophane bags among the bags the ties close but says nothing about food-contact certification [6]. If your tie touches only the outside of the bag, as Tractor Supply advises [7], the question is smaller than if the tie were inside. Nothing here was research whether any food authority requires a certificate for an external tie; ask your supplier and your local authority.

## A bench test before you order a case

- Order a sample pack of two bag sizes, or five of each tie length, rather than a case.

- Fill each bag with your real product to the weight you plan to sell. Fold the top the same number of times each time, and bend the tie.

- Hold the closed bag upside down and shake gently; look for crumbs or powder at the fold.

- Carry a few bags in a tote or a box for a day, then check whether any fold has sprung open.

- Open and reclose each bag ten times. Note whether the tie cracks the paper at the fold or the cover splits.

- Leave one bag open for a day beside one tied and one heat-sealed bag; compare crispness at day 3 and day 7. That window is a guess at a useful test, not a figure from a source.

Write the result down for each closure: bag, size, liner, tie, folds, outcome. The table you end up with is your own packing specification, and it is the cheapest quality control you will buy.

## Questions to ask the supplier

- What are the exact pocket dimensions, and what weight and product was the label rating based on?

- What is the liner: none, polyethylene, PET or PLA, and is it heat sealable?

- Is the tie metal, and is it covered in paper or plastic? What is its length and width?

- Is the paper free of added PFAS? One supplier's pages state "no PFAS added" for California [2].

- If the bag is sold as compostable, does the certificate cover the tie and the liner?

- Can I get a small sample before a case?

## Common mistakes

- Choosing by pound label. The same label means different dimensions at different suppliers [2][3].

- Treating the tie as a seal. Sellers disagree, and a reviewer advises against storing coffee in them [2][5].

- Too few folds. Our observation, not a sourced fact: a short fold springs open.

- Paper ties in damp conditions. They are much less water resistant than plastic ties [6].

- Ties that are too short. Leave extra overlap [6].

## FAQ

### Are tin tie bags reusable?

They are reclosable by design; the tie can be opened and closed again [1]. Paper folds wear out.

### Can I heat-seal a tin tie bag?

Pactiv says its tin tie coffee bags can be heat sealed [1]; check each listing.

### What length twist tie do I need?

Suppliers sell 4 to 10 inches and advise leaving enough overlap [6]. Test your own neck.

### Is a metal twist tie safe on food bags?

Interplas does not claim food certification [6]. Keep the tie on the outside of the bag [7] and ask your supplier.
