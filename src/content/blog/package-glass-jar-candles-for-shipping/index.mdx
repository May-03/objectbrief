> **Quick answer.** A glass jar candle needs a packaging system, not merely a branded box. The practical goal is to keep a filled vessel, its lid, and its label stable from the packing bench to the customer while keeping the pack reasonable to buy, store, and ship. For a small brand, the first decision is whether a product box is only for presentation or is expected to take the handling loads of parcel delivery. That distinction determines the box structure, the insert, the outer carton, and the sample test you need before ordering a production run.

This guide is for small brands shipping finished candles in glass jars or similarly fragile vessels. It does not certify a package or set a universal damage-free design. A jar's geometry, packed weight, route, order mix, carrier handling, climate, and the actual board and insert supplied all change the result. Treat the steps below as a buying and validation method.

## Start With the Shipment Rather Than the Box

A shelf carton can make a candle look complete and giftable, yet still be unsuitable as the only parcel package. A parcel package must manage motion, impact and compression. The same candle may therefore use a retail carton inside a protective mailer, or a single corrugated mailer with an insert when the brand wants one pack to do both jobs. The right choice follows the order pattern, not the visual style of the product.

FedEx's fragile-item guidance recommends centering a fragile item in a sturdy box, restricting movement with cushioning, and using a box-in-box approach with 3 inches of cushioning around the inner box for breakable shipments. That is a carrier recommendation, not a candle-specific design rule or a substitute for testing your actual package. [1]

| Sales and fulfilment situation | Useful packaging starting point | What still has to be checked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retail shelf or local hand delivery | A fitted folding carton or rigid gift box can be the presentation layer. | Whether the candle can move, rub its label, or chip when carried with other stock. |
| One glass jar shipped directly to a customer | A mailer or outer corrugated carton with a fitted paperboard insert or controlled cushioning. | Clearance to every wall, lid security, packed weight, and the cost of dimensional shipping. |
| Two or more jars in one order | A divided tray or individual cells inside an outer carton. | Every jar must be separated; one jar must not become the impact surface for another. |
| Gift set with a premium unboxing requirement | Retail gift box plus a separate outer shipping carton, unless the whole pack is validated as a mailer. | Whether the gift box stays clean and the insert restrains every component. |

## Measure the Packed Candle Before Requesting a Quote

Do not order a "small candle box" from a catalogue and then try to make the jar fit. Suppliers need the actual packed unit. Measure the widest outside diameter or width of the vessel, its total height with the lid fitted, and every feature that changes the envelope: a rim, base foot, cap overhang, raised label, handle, or wick protector. Also record the filled weight. Volume alone is not a box dimension.

For a retail carton, the supplier needs the product envelope and the intended presentation. For a shipping pack, they also need the proposed restraint method and the outer-carton arrangement. A die-cut insert, divider, wrap, and loose-fill solution all consume space differently. Ask the supplier to identify which dimensions on the quotation are internal and which are external; quoting one without the other causes avoidable mistakes.

| Send to the packaging supplier | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Vessel drawing or photographs from front, side and base | Shows a taper, foot, shoulder, or lid detail that a diameter alone can miss. |
| Maximum diameter or width and maximum height with lid | Defines the true package envelope. |
| Finished packed weight | Affects board, insert, carton and test decisions. |
| One, two, four or more units per typical order | Determines whether individual cells or dividers are needed. |
| Retail-only, parcel-only, or both | Prevents a display carton from being quoted as if it were transport packaging. |
| Photos of an existing damaged shipment, if any | Gives the supplier a defined failure mode to address. |

## Use the Insert to Control Movement

Loose fill occupies empty space; an insert or divider positions the product. For a single jar, a die-cut paperboard or corrugated insert can hold the base and sides so the candle cannot slide or tip easily. For several jars, a divider gives each vessel its own cell and prevents glass-to-glass contact. A wrap may protect the surface and rim, but it is not automatically a positioning system once there is still room inside the carton.

Corrugated cushioning does not behave like a permanent universal buffer. Research on corrugated fibreboard shows that impact behaviour and repeated-impact performance depend on the board and its compression condition. [2] [3] That is one reason to ask what the proposed insert is made from and to test the supplied sample rather than assuming every "corrugated insert" performs the same way.

The simplest inspection is the movement check. Close the trial pack and move it gently through the orientations it will see in handling. Any audible rattle, side-to-side travel, lid contact with the outer wall, or jar-to-jar contact means the restraint design needs work. Add filler only to close residual gaps; do not use progressively more filler to hide an oversized box.

## Choose One Packaging Architecture for Each Order Pattern

Small brands often create unnecessary cost by making one expensive structure perform every job. A useful first architecture is to separate the retail layer from the transport layer. The candle enters a clean retail carton or sleeve; that unit is then placed in a plain corrugated shipping carton with the appropriate clearance and cushioning. This works well when retail and direct-to-consumer orders coexist, because the presentation pack is not asked to absorb every transport event.

A combined corrugated mailer with a fitted insert is useful when a single jar ships mainly through parcel networks and the brand accepts a more functional unboxing. The key question is not whether the box has a logo. It is whether the vessel is fixed, the box remains square under its loaded weight, and the design leaves suitable protection around the product. For multi-jar orders, use a cell or divider layout designed around the actual jar diameter, height and count. Do not let the customer's order quantity decide the spacing on the packing bench.

## Run a Controlled Sample Test Before a Production Order

Order samples before committing to printed packaging. Build the package exactly as it would leave the business: finished candle, lid, label, insert, outer carton, tape, and the same packing sequence. Photograph the packed configuration and record each component. Then inspect after handling tests and after a sample shipment to the farthest or most demanding normal destination.

A small in-house test can reveal obvious movement and corner weakness, but it does not recreate every route or claim compliance with a transport standard. For more formal evaluation, ISTA publishes test procedures that include drop and vibration elements and identifies Procedure 3A as a general-simulation procedure for individual parcel shipments up to 150 lb (70 kg). [4] Use a qualified laboratory and the current procedure when a retailer, insurer, customer, or internal quality plan requires formal validation.

- Pack at least one representative sample for every order pattern: single unit, common multi-unit order, and gift set if applicable.
- Record the filled weight, external dimensions, insert material, carton construction stated by the supplier, tape pattern, and packing sequence.
- Inspect the jar, rim, lid, wax surface, label, box corners, insert deformation, and internal movement after each check.
- Change one variable at a time: box size, insert geometry, divider, wrap, or cushioning. Otherwise the result cannot tell you what improved the package.

## Compare Total Packaging Cost Rather Than Price Per Box

The lowest unit price can produce the highest delivered cost if it creates excess void space, increases parcel dimensions, requires large quantities of filler, or produces damaged orders. Build a simple per-order comparison before selecting a supplier:

Pack cost per order = retail pack + insert or divider + outer carton + wrap and void fill + tape and label + expected replacement cost

For a new design, expected replacement cost is uncertain. Do not invent a damage rate. Track the number of damaged orders and the full cost of reshipment after launch, then compare packaging versions over similar routes and order sizes. Storage matters too: a flat-packed custom carton can be practical at one order volume and burdensome at another. Ask each supplier for carton pack quantity, flat-pack dimensions, sample cost, tooling or plate cost, lead time, and whether changes to the jar require a new insert or dieline.

## Use This RFQ Before Comparing Offers

- Please quote a sample using the attached jar dimensions, filled weight, lid, and photo. State the internal and external box dimensions.
- Please identify the board construction and the insert or divider material, thickness, layout, and the amount of side, base, and top clearance in the sample.
- Please quote separately for plain sample, printed production run, insert or divider, tooling or dieline, packing quantity, lead time, and shipping terms.
- Please show whether the sample is designed for one jar, a defined multi-jar count, or a retail carton inside an outer shipper.
- Please confirm what will change if the jar diameter, lid height, packed weight, or order count changes.

A supplier can quote a box quickly. The useful quotation is the one that makes the product, restraint method, dimensions, material, order pattern, and sample test visible. That gives a small candle brand a basis for choosing packaging that is both presentable and defensible in real fulfilment.
