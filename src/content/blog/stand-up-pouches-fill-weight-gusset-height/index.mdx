> **Quick answer.** Pouch dimensions run width by height by gusset, but only the space below the zipper or seal line holds product. One supplier guide says to subtract about an inch from the height for a zipper, and ePac advises filling to roughly 80 percent of capacity so the top inch stays clean for sealing. Size charts disagree because density does: one seller lists the same medium pouch at 4 oz of tea, 6 oz of whole coffee and 13 oz of flour. Treat any chart as a starting point, order about ten sample pouches in two sizes, and fill them with your actual product on your actual scale.

## Read the three numbers the right way round

Stand-up pouch sizes are written width by height by gusset. Carepac gives the order explicitly [6], and Pouchespack's example, 6 by 9 by 3 inches, means 6 inches wide, 9 inches tall and a 3-inch bottom gusset [11]. The gusset is the flat depth of the base when the pouch is opened. Carepac adds a useful test: if a listing shows no gusset, it is a two-seal or three-seal pouch and will not stand on the shelf [6].

Height is where buyers go wrong. The listed height runs from the bottom seal to the top seal, but product only sits below the zipper or the seal line. ePac says to measure usable height from the base of the gusset to the seal line or zipper, because only the space below it can hold product [2]. Carepac makes the same point: zipper and seal components take up room, and the fillable area is the section below the zipper [6]. Kimecopak puts a number on it, subtracting about an inch from the total height for the fill line when there is a zipper [5], and YLTPACK says the same, adding that space must be left above the zipper line for the tear notch [8].

Our arithmetic: an inch is a small allowance on a large bag and a big one on a small bag. Taken off a 9-inch pouch it removes about 11 percent of the height; taken off a 6-inch pouch it removes about 17 percent. So a small pouch loses proportionally more capacity to its closure than a large one, and a chart built on large sizes will flatter a small one.

## The volume formula is an upper bound

ePac treats a stand-up pouch as a rectangular prism: width times usable height times gusset depth. Its example, 15 cm by 20 cm by 6 cm, gives a nominal 1,800 cm³ [2]. The same page says this is a nominal figure and recommends a physical fill test for airy products such as granola and for dense products, where the filled sample is poured into a measuring container [2].

The caution is worth taking seriously, because an opened pouch is not a box. We compared the formula with one seller's stated capacities. MakeStickers lists three standard sizes, 4.375 by 6 by 2 inches, 5.75 by 7.5 by 2.5 inches and 7 by 9 by 3 inches, with total volumes of 6, 18 and 32 oz [4]. Multiplying out the dimensions gives about 860, 1,770 and 3,100 cm³. If the listed ounces are fluid ounces, which the page does not say, the stated volumes are about 177, 532 and 946 mL, only about 21 to 31 percent of the prism figure. We cannot tell whether the seller's unit is fluid or weight, so read this as a sign that the prism overstates the space, not as a measurement.

|Size (W x H x G, in)|Prism, W x H x G (our arithmetic)|Seller total volume [4]|Listed volume as % of prism, if fluid oz|
|---|---|---|---|
|4.375 x 6 x 2|about 860 cm³|6 oz|about 21%|
|5.75 x 7.5 x 2.5|about 1,770 cm³|18 oz|about 30%|
|7 x 9 x 3|about 3,100 cm³|32 oz|about 31%|

The prism figure uses the full listed height, so it is an upper bound before the zipper allowance. The seller does not define its ounce; the last column assumes fluid ounces at 29.57 mL each.

## Why one chart cannot size your pouch

Weight tells you little until density comes in. Uprinting says plainly that different products of the same weight fill a pouch differently and that you need your product's volume [3]. Kimecopak says 500 g of coffee is not 500 g of whey protein, one being dense and the other fluffy [5]. Pouchespack says a pouch holding 500 g of salt may not hold 500 g of oats, and that filling sample pouches is the only reliable test [11].

The MakeStickers table shows how wide the spread is. Its medium pouch is listed at 4 oz of tea, 6 oz of whole coffee beans, 10 oz of whole almonds, 12 oz of gummy bears and 13 oz of flour [4]. That is more than a threefold difference in weight for one bag.

|Source|Pouch|Coffee beans|Notes|
|---|---|---|---|
|Uprinting [3]|7.25 x 6 x 2 in|5 oz|Listed as 1.5 cups|
|Uprinting [3]|8.125 x 10 x 3.5 in|1 lb 8 oz|Listed as 8.5 cups|
|MakeStickers [4]|5.75 x 7.5 x 2.5 in|6 oz|Small is 2 oz, large is 11 oz|
|Ecopackables [10]|12 oz bag, listed 355 ml|about 300 to 340 g whole bean|Different volume basis|

Values as stated in each source; none is a measurement of ours. The Uprinting figures imply roughly 0.34 to 0.40 g per mL of whole beans (the arithmetic here from the cups and ounces listed).

Read the last row against the first. Ecopackables lists a 12 oz bag as 355 ml holding about 300 to 340 g of whole beans, which works out to roughly 0.85 to 0.96 g per mL; Uprinting's rows imply about 0.34 to 0.40 [3][10]. Both cannot describe the same internal volume. We suspect the Ecopackables column labels the bag by its ounce rating rather than its true cavity, but that is the reading here. The practical point is that a "volume" column in one chart cannot be compared with another chart without knowing what it measures.

Grind and shape matter within one product. Belmark's flat-bottom box pouch chart lists a 12 to 14 oz fill of ground coffee in a 5.5 by 9.5 by 3 inch bag and the same weight of whole bean in a 6 by 10 by 3 inch bag, a slightly larger one [9]. That is a different pouch style from a stand-up pouch, so the dimensions are not transferable, but the direction is useful: the fluffier fill needs more space for the same weight.

## Leave room for the seal

ePac's guide says to fill to about 80 percent of total capacity so there is headspace that keeps product out of the seal zone, and to keep the top inch clear from the first scoop [1]. It also calls residue in the seal area the leading cause of leaks and spoilage, and advises wiping the seal area and spot-checking seals with a gentle squeeze or burst test [1]. The page is slightly inconsistent, since its FAQ says roughly three-quarters [1], so treat 75 to 80 percent as a starting range and not a rule.

A zipper does not replace the top seal. Vivo Packaging notes that commercially packed products are commonly sealed above the zipper before sale, and that the zipper and sealing area need space above the contents [7]. If your product is sold sealed, the zipper is for the customer's second opening, and your fill line has to sit below both. Uprinting adds that products sold with a scoop need extra headspace for a secure seal [3].

Powders and liquids behave differently. ePac says powders should go in slowly through a narrow funnel because dust can settle in the seal area and weaken the bond, and that liquids need a funnel or pump with the fill level well below the seal [1]. Fragile items should be lowered in rather than poured, so nothing is crushed at the seal [1]. If your line fills by auger, the page on [tabletop auger fillers for spice pouches] covers the dosing side, and [impulse versus band sealers] covers the closing step.

## Choosing the size from a fill test

- Weigh your finished product to the target, for example 340 g, and note whether it is ground, whole, or loose.

- Order a few sample pouches in two neighbouring sizes. Sticker Mule says you can start with as few as 10 pouches to find the most accurate capacity for your product [13].

- Fill one pouch of each size with the target weight, tap it to settle, and look at where the product sits against the zipper.

- Measure the clear space from the top of the product to the top of the pouch. Compare it with the guidance above: about an inch of clear seal zone, plus the zipper.

- Stand the filled pouch upright for a day. Check that it stands, that the front panel is smooth enough for a label, and that the gusset has not bulged outwards.

- Squeeze gently to test the closed pouch, then repeat on five more. Record any that leak or show product in the seal.

Write down the pouch size, the product, the fill weight, the settled height and the clear space. That record is your spec for reorders and for the supplier, and it is worth more than any chart.

## Planning a size ladder across several products

Most small brands sell more than one weight, and each extra pouch size is another minimum order. Two printers show what a standard ladder looks like. 4OVER4 lists typical custom sizes from about 3.5 by 5 inches for a 1 to 2 oz sample up to 12 by 18 inches for a 5 lb bulk fill, with 4 oz at 5 by 8 inches, 8 oz at 6 by 9 inches and 1 lb at 8 by 12 by 4 inches [14]. Kimecopak's coffee chart pairs 250 g with 4 by 6 by 2 inches, 500 g with 6 by 8 by 3 inches and 1 kg with 8 by 12 by 4 inches [5]. These are catalogue pairings from sellers and neither says how it was tested.

Put the two ladders side by side and they already differ: the 8 by 12 by 4 inch pouch is a 1 lb size at one printer and a 1 kg coffee size at the other [5][14]. That is a spread of about 550 g between the two (1 kg against 1 lb, the arithmetic here), which is the density question again. Our suggestion, and it is only a suggestion, is to choose one gusset depth across your range where you can, test each weight in the smallest pouch that closes cleanly, and drop any size that two of your products would fill differently enough to need separate bags.

## Gusset, label panel and shelf behaviour

The gusset sets how the pouch stands. A deeper gusset gives more volume and a steadier base, but it also changes the shape of the front panel when full. Vistaprint notes that capacity is affected by zipper closures, seal sizes, tear notches and hang holes, and that product size and shape influence fit [12]. If you plan a front label or a printed window, check how wide the flat front is once the pouch is full; a filled pouch is likely to bow, which is our expectation and not a sourced figure. Our page on [coffee bag label applicators] covers applying labels to the finished bag.

The reading here of all this is that weight is the claim you print and volume is the constraint you buy, so never size from one without the other. If you sell by weight, state the net weight you actually deliver, and check your local rules on net contents labelling and on slack in packages. We have not researched those rules here.

## Questions to ask the pouch supplier

- What are the exact dimensions, and is the height measured to the top edge or to the seal or zipper line?

- How far below the top edge is the zipper, and how wide is the seal zone above it?

- What fill volume or weight does the supplier recommend for my product, and is it from a test or a chart?

- Is there a tear notch, a hang hole, or a valve, and do they take space from the fillable area?

- How many samples can I buy, and are they the same film and zipper as the production run?

- What is the tolerance on width, height and gusset between lots?

## Common mistakes

- Ordering by the weight on a chart. Density decides the fit [3][4][5].

- Measuring to the top edge. Only the space below the zipper holds product [2][6].

- Filling to the zipper. Leave a clean seal zone, about the top inch [1].

- Trusting the prism formula. It is nominal and overstates real capacity [2].

- Comparing volume columns across charts. They may measure different things [3][10].

## FAQ

### How full should a stand-up pouch be?

About 75 to 80 percent of capacity, according to ePac's guide, so the seal zone stays clean [1].

### Does a 12 oz pouch hold 12 oz?

Not necessarily. It holds what your product's density allows; one source lists a 12 oz pouch as holding about 300 to 340 g of whole coffee beans and less ground [10].

### Why does the same pouch hold more flour than tea?

Density and shape. One seller lists the same medium pouch at 13 oz of flour and 4 oz of tea [4].

### Do I need the gusset?

If you want it to stand, yes. A listing with no gusset is not a stand-up pouch [6].
