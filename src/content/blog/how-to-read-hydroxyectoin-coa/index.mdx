> **Quick answer.** A Hydroxyectoin COA should be read field by field and method by method. A "99% HPLC" headline does not prove low residual Ectoin, does not confirm identity, and does not on its own establish a fully defined specification. Before approving a supplier grade, separate identity from purity, confirm the method can distinguish Hydroxyectoin from Ectoin, ask whether related Ectoin is reported separately, and verify the document belongs to the lot you are buying.

A Hydroxyectoin supplier can write "99% HPLC" in a quotation, a specification or even a COA. That number looks precise. It is not yet a complete quality statement.

Before approving the material, a buyer still needs to know what the 99% field means, how Hydroxyectoin was identified, whether the chromatographic method can distinguish Hydroxyectoin from Ectoin, whether related Ectoin is reported separately, and whether the document belongs to the lot being purchased.

The practical rule is simple:

A Hydroxyectoin COA should be read field by field and method by method. Do not treat the headline purity number as a substitute for the analytical package.

## Step 1 — Ask What "99% HPLC" Actually Means

The first question is not whether 99% sounds high enough.

Ask how that 99% was calculated.

A supplier may use "HPLC purity" to describe a chromatographic peak-area percentage. Another may report a calibrated quantitative assay. A third may use a supplier-specific calculation or basis.

Those are not interchangeable.

ICH Q2(R2) and Q14 are pharmaceutical guidelines rather than cosmetic raw-material regulations, but the analytical principle is useful: an analytical procedure should be suitable for the attribute it is intended to measure, with appropriate specificity or selectivity, accuracy, precision and range.[1][2]

For a Hydroxyectoin COA, the buyer should therefore ask:

- Is the field called purity, assay or content?
- Is the result area-normalized?
- Is an external or internal reference standard used?
- Is the result reported as-is, on dried basis or on another basis?
- What does the method count as Hydroxyectoin?

If the supplier cannot explain the basis of the 99% result, the number should not be treated as a fully defined quantitative specification.

## Step 2 — Separate Identity From Purity

A high HPLC number does not answer every identity question.

Before discussing purity, confirm that the tested material is Hydroxyectoin: HYDROXYECTOIN, CAS 165542-15-4.

Identity can be supported in different ways depending on the analytical package. Research methods have used retention-time comparison against authentic standards, while other analytical contexts may use mass spectrometry or spectroscopic techniques.

The key point is not that every cosmetic supplier must use one particular identity method.

The key point is that identity and purity answer different questions.

A chromatogram may show one dominant peak, but the COA should still make clear why that peak is assigned to Hydroxyectoin.

For second-source qualification, keep identity checks tied to the exact material record:

product name → INCI → CAS → grade → specification → batch COA.

Do not approve a new source simply because its product title says "Hydroxyectoin 99%."

## Step 3 — Check Whether the Method Can Separate Ectoin and Hydroxyectoin

This is the most important Hydroxyectoin-specific question.

Ectoin and Hydroxyectoin are structurally related compounds, but analytical literature shows that they can be separated and measured as distinct analytes when the chromatographic method is designed for that purpose.

García-Estepa and colleagues used HPLC to analyze Ectoin and Hydroxyectoin separately, with commercially available reference materials used to establish retention times.[3]

More recent work has likewise quantified the two compounds separately with HPLC-UV and external standards.[4] A 2026 Frontiers study also used a dedicated chromatographic method and reference samples to quantify Ectoin and Hydroxyectoin independently.[6]

That evidence establishes an important buyer principle:

"HPLC" is not enough information by itself.

The method must have adequate selectivity for the analytical question.

If a supplier claims Hydroxyectoin purity but Ectoin is a decision-relevant related substance, ask whether the release method resolves the two compounds and how the Ectoin peak is identified.

Do not assume that any HPLC method automatically does this.

## Step 4 — Do Not Convert 99% Hydroxyectoin Into "Ectoin ≤ 1%"

This is a common arithmetic shortcut, and it is not justified by the headline number alone.

Suppose a COA says:

Hydroxyectoin purity: 99.0% HPLC.

It does not automatically follow that:

Ectoin: ≤ 1.0%.

The unaccounted fraction is not automatically Ectoin. Depending on the method and calculation basis, it may include other organic peaks, unidentified peaks, degradation-related peaks or other chromatographically visible components.

And a chromatographic purity result may not describe water or inorganic material at all.

The correct buyer question is therefore separate:

Is Ectoin a named related substance with its own specification and lot result?

That question is particularly relevant because Ectoin can be a process-related precursor in some Hydroxyectoin production strategies. Jungmann and colleagues demonstrated Hydroxyectoin production through Ectoin hydroxylation, including a two-stage process in which Ectoin was first produced and then converted to Hydroxyectoin.[5]

That does not mean every commercial Hydroxyectoin lot contains high residual Ectoin.

It means residual Ectoin is analytically plausible enough that a buyer should request a specific field if it matters to the approved material profile.

## Step 5 — Read "Assay," "Purity" and "Related Ectoin" as Separate Fields

A strong COA structure makes those concepts difficult to confuse.

For example, a buyer may want to see fields such as:

| Field | What the buyer needs to know |
| --- | --- |
| Identity | Does the method confirm Hydroxyectoin? |
| Assay / content | How much Hydroxyectoin is quantitatively present, and on what basis? |
| HPLC purity | What proportion of the defined chromatographic signal is assigned to the main component? |
| Related Ectoin | Is Ectoin identified and controlled separately? |
| Loss on Drying | What mass is lost under the specified drying conditions? |
| pH | At what test-solution concentration and method? |
| Microbiology / metals | Are the required controls appropriate for the intended supply chain? |

Not every supplier will use exactly these labels, and no buyer should invent a universal Hydroxyectoin specification.

The point is to understand what each field proves.

A supplier with "99% HPLC" plus a separately reported Ectoin result is providing more decision-useful information than a supplier whose only analytical field is one undefined 99% number.

## Step 6 — Do Not Rename Loss on Drying as Water

Hydroxyectoin specifications may include Loss on Drying.

That field should be read literally.

Loss on Drying measures the mass lost under defined drying conditions. It is not automatically a water-specific determination.

This is the same analytical distinction already used elsewhere in Object Brief's Ectoin series: LOD and water content are different concepts.

If moisture is important to formulation dosing, storage or incoming control, ask whether the supplier has a water-specific method in addition to or instead of LOD.

Do not calculate an "as-is Hydroxyectoin content" from a dried-basis number unless the analytical bases are actually documented.

## Step 7 — Treat pH as a Test Method, Not a Formula Recommendation

A raw-material COA may report pH for an aqueous Hydroxyectoin solution.

That value is useful only with the method context.

Ask:

- What concentration was tested?
- What solvent was used?
- At what temperature?
- Which method or instrument procedure applies?

Do not convert a raw-material solution pH into a finished-formula stability range.

A COA pH result is an incoming-quality measurement. It is not automatically a formulation recommendation.

This is especially important during supplier switches: two suppliers can report different pH values simply because their test concentrations or methods differ.

## Step 8 — Verify That the COA Belongs to the Lot You Are Buying

Public and sample COAs are useful during discovery.

They can show which fields a supplier normally reports and whether the supplier appears to understand related-substance control.

They do not release your shipment.

Before approval, match the lot-specific COA to:

- supplier legal identity
- exact product / grade
- batch or lot number
- current specification
- purchase order or shipment
- current document revision where applicable

A research-reagent COA is also not a shortcut to cosmetic qualification.

A reagent supplier may provide excellent identity and HPLC data, but those documents do not automatically establish the microbiological, regulatory, traceability, packaging or change-control requirements expected for a cosmetic raw-material supply chain.

## Step 9 — Compare Second Sources Method-to-Method, Not Number-to-Number

Suppose Supplier A and Supplier B both quote Hydroxyectoin 99%.

That does not yet mean the grades are analytically equivalent.

Build a method-level comparison:

Supplier A: 99% HPLC purity, related Ectoin reported separately, defined identity method.

Supplier B: 99% HPLC, no calculation basis disclosed, no named Ectoin field.

Those two "99%" results do not carry the same information.

For a second-source review, compare:

- field name
- specification limit
- method
- calculation basis
- reference standard
- identity evidence
- related Ectoin control
- water / LOD basis
- pH test condition
- current lot result

If a field is missing, record it as a data gap rather than filling it with a value from another supplier.

## When Should You Ask for the Chromatogram or Method Summary?

Not every purchase requires a full analytical-method package. But a headline COA is sometimes insufficient for the decision.

Ask for more method detail when:

- the supplier reports only "99% HPLC" with no calculation basis;
- related Ectoin is important to your material specification but is not listed separately;
- two suppliers report similar purity but use different field names;
- a new lot shows an unexpected change in color, pH, dissolution or finished-formula behavior;
- the supplier changes production route, analytical laboratory or specification revision;
- your internal test and the supplier COA disagree materially.

The first escalation does not have to be a complete validation dossier. A method summary, representative chromatogram, system-suitability information or explanation of how Ectoin is identified may be enough to resolve the question.

What matters is traceability between the claim and the analytical procedure.

If the supplier says Ectoin is controlled, ask where that control appears: as a named peak, a separate quantitative test, a related-substances limit or another documented criterion.

If the supplier cannot connect the claim to a method and a specification field, record the issue as unresolved rather than treating the marketing description as analytical evidence.

## A Practical Hydroxyectoin COA Approval Sequence

For an incoming Hydroxyectoin candidate, use this order:

1. Confirm identity and grade.
2. Identify what the headline HPLC percentage actually represents.
3. Confirm the method can distinguish Hydroxyectoin from relevant related compounds.
4. Check whether Ectoin has a named specification and lot result.
5. Read LOD, water and pH only according to their stated methods.
6. Compare the batch COA against the current specification.
7. Resolve document conflicts before approving the lot.

The most important question is not:

"Is the purity 99%?"

It is:

"What exactly has this COA measured, and is that evidence sufficient for the material specification I am approving?"
