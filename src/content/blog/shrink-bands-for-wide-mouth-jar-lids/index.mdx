> **Quick answer.** For an 89 mm lid, sellers point to bands with a lay-flat width of 152 to 155 mm. For a 110 mm lid, look at about 190 mm, and for 120 mm, 205 to 215 mm. Heights run from 28 mm, which covers the join between lid and jar, to 55 mm and 95 mm, which wrap much of the jar and protect a label. On lids this wide, a preform band with a small lip is easier to keep in place than a flat one. Measure the outside of your own lid, since the thread size understates it, and expect to turn the jar while you heat.

Two cautions apply to every row. The lid size in a product name, such as 89-400, is the thread. The outside of the lid is wider, and lids with deep ribs or a domed top are wider still. And one catalogue's "fits" range is not another's. A retailer describes its 155 x 55 mm band as fitting jars from 2.6 to 3.7 inches across [4], a far wider range than the stock catalogue gives for the same width [3]. Both can be true, because film shrinks a long way. Use the table to pick a starting size, then check it against your own lid.

## Why is the fit range so wide on big bands?

Because the shrink is proportional and the band is large. Craft suppliers say their bands shrink by up to 40 percent of their original size [5]. A 155 mm band opens to a circle about 99 mm across. Forty percent of that is nearly 40 mm, so in principle the band can close onto anything from 99 mm down to about 60 mm. That is how one seller can quote 2.6 to 3.7 inches for a single band [4].

In practice you do not want to use all of that range. A band shrinking a long way thickens, wrinkles and takes longer to heat evenly. A distributor's rule of thumb points to a closer fit: halve the circumference of the lid and add 2 mm [6]. For a lid that measures 92 mm across the outside, that gives about 147 mm, and the nearest stock sizes above it are 152 and 155 mm. The less the film has to travel, the neater the result.

## How tall should the band be?

This is the main choice on a wide jar, and there are three common answers.

About 28 to 33 mm: a rim band. It bridges the edge of the lid and the top of the jar. Stock catalogues list every large width at 28 mm [3], and preform bands for 89 mm lids are 33 mm plus a 7 mm lip [2]. This is the least film, the lowest cost and the least interference with a label.

About 55 mm: a half wrap. Craft suppliers sell most of their jar bands at this height [1][4]. On a short jar it reaches well down the side. One buyer uses a 155 x 55 mm band on 12 oz short jars with 89/400 lids mainly to protect a kraft paper label, and notes that it covers the body of the jar only [1]. That is worth reading twice. A band that sits on the body and does not reach the lid protects the label and shows nothing about opening.

About 95 mm: a full wrap. One supplier recommends a 155 x 95 mm band for its 16 oz straight-wall jars and 8 oz double-wall jars [7]. This covers lid, join and most of the jar in one sleeve.

To decide, measure from the top edge of the lid down to where you want the film to stop. If the purpose is a tamper signal, the band must cross the join between lid and jar with film on both sides of it. A distributor suggests about 5 mm of overhang past the closure at each end [6]; on a wide lid that means a little film folded onto the top of the lid and a few millimetres of grip on the jar below it.

## Flat band or preform?

A flat band is a plain loop. A preform band has one edge turned inward at the factory, written as an extra figure: 152 x 33 + 7 mm means a 7 mm lip [2]. The lip rests on the lid and stops the band sliding down the jar before it has shrunk.

Preforms are common in exactly this size range. One supplier lists a 152 x 33 + 7 mm preform for 89 mm caps and lids [2], and a distributor sells the same size by the case, pictured on the lid of a one-gallon container, alongside a 165 x 30 + 7 mm version [8]. Our page on deli containers describes how buyers find the lip helpful on lids of about 117 mm.

There is a trade-off. The preform bands the sources reviewed show for 89 mm lids are sold as non-perforated [2]. If your customer needs to tear the band off by hand, check for a perforated version or choose a flat perforated band and accept that you will need to hold it in place for the first second of heat.

## How do you shrink a band this big?

Suppliers' directions are the usual ones: place the band on the container and shrink it with a heat gun or hair dryer [9]. One buyer of 153 mm bands uses a heat embossing tool from a craft kit, working gently around the lid [10]. The steps below are suggestions for large lids and have not been tested.

- Screw the lid down fully. On a wide lid, a quarter turn of slack is easy to miss and the band will lock it in.

- Stand the jar on something you can spin: a cake turntable, a lazy Susan or an upturned bowl.

- Position the band. With a flat band, hold it level with a fingertip on the far side.

- Start the heat at one point on the lower edge, then spin the jar slowly so the heat travels all the way round before you move upward. Heating one side fully first pulls the band off-centre.

- Make a second lap at the top edge so the film folds onto the lid.

- Stop when the film is tight. On thick-walled plastic jars, do not hold the heat in one place.

A wide band has a long seam and a long perforation. If either opens during shrinking, the heat was probably too concentrated. See [shrink band problems and fixes](/blog/shrink-band-problems-and-fixes).

## Three worked examples

The jars below are illustrations with made-up dimensions, to show how the choices combine. Substitute your own measurements.

A sugar scrub in a short 8 oz jar, lid 92 mm across, label on the side wall starting 20 mm below the lid. The formula gives about 147 mm, so a 152 or 155 mm band. The label rules out a 55 mm band, which would cover its top edge. A 28 mm rim band, or a 33 mm preform, sits in the clear space.

A bath salt in a tall straight-sided jar with a paper label you want protected. Here the long band is the point. A 155 x 95 mm sleeve of the kind one supplier sells for straight-wall jars [7] covers the label and crosses the lid join in one piece. Check that the perforation runs the full height so the customer can remove it.

A dry mix in a tub with a lid about 110 mm across. The stock catalogue's 190 x 28 mm band is rated for caps of 4 1/4 to 4 1/2 inches [3]. At this diameter a flat band is awkward to hold level, so use a turntable, or look for a preform in a nearby size.

## Label under the film or clear of it?

A band over a label protects it from scuffs and damp hands, as the buyer with kraft labels found [1]. It also means the label is seen through film until the band is torn off, with a seam and a perforation line running across it. If the label carries small print that a shopper needs to read at the shelf, keep the band above it. If the label is mostly a logo, a tall clear band over it does no harm.

## Is a band the right seal for a wide-mouth jar?

Often, but ask what you need it to do. A band shows that a jar has been opened. It does not seal the mouth. Powders, scrubs and butters in wide jars are frequently given an inner seal as well: a pressure-sensitive or induction liner under the lid, or a plastic sealing disc. Our comparison of [shrink bands vs induction seals] sets out when the inner seal matters more.

If your wide-mouth product is a food or a cosmetic sold in the United States, the sources reviewed show no general federal rule requiring tamper-evident packaging for it, with narrow exceptions described in [are shrink bands required by law](/blog/are-shrink-bands-required-by-law). Supplements in powder form raise their own questions, which nothing here was research here.

## Common problems on wide lids

- The band drops to the shoulder. Nothing held it up. Use a preform, or pinch it at the rim until the first heat grips.

- It shrinks into a tilted ring. One side was heated to completion before the other. Spin the jar.

- It covers the label. The band is taller than the gap above your label. Choose 28 mm, or move the label down.

- It grips the jar but the lid still turns. The band sits below the lid, as in the label-protection review above [1]. Raise it so it crosses the join.

- The top edge stands up like a collar. Too much overhang on a flat lid. A second pass of heat across the top usually lays it down; if not, use a shorter band.

These are practical inferences from the sources and from how film behaves, not test results.

## Measure before you order

- Wrap a paper strip around the widest part of the closed lid. Mark the overlap and measure the length.

- Halve that length and add 2 mm [6]. This is the smallest lay-flat width to consider.

- Measure from the lid top to where the band should end. Add about 5 mm for the fold onto the lid.

- Check the gap between the lid and the top of your label.

- Pick the nearest stock size at or above your width, in a height that clears the label.

- Order one pack and band five jars before buying more.

## FAQ

### Will a wide-mouth Mason jar band fit an 89 mm cosmetic jar?

Wide-mouth canning lids are a little smaller than 89 mm. One marketplace band of 153 x 28 mm is sold for both canisters and wide-mouth Mason jars [10], so there is overlap. Measure your lid.

### Can I use a hair dryer on a band this large?

Suppliers allow it [9]. There is more film to heat, so it will be slow. A heat gun on low is quicker and more even.

### Do I need a 55 mm band if a 28 mm band fits?

Only if you want the film to protect a label or cover more of the jar.

### Are these bands PVC?

The stock and craft bands cited here are described as PVC at 2 mil or 200 gauge [3][4]. Some marketplace bands in large sizes are PETG.

### Do square or oval tubs use the same sizes?

They are sized the same way, by the distance around the lid. Use the paper strip.
