> **Quick answer.** A tabletop auger filler can be a sensible fit for a small operation packing one or a few dry spice powders into pre-made pouches. But a listing that says "5-100 g" does not establish that your specific 100 g blend will dose consistently, reach your required output, or stay out of the pouch seal area. Treat 100 g as a test point, not a performance guarantee.

Before paying for a machine, send the supplier the actual spice blend and pouch, then request a witnessed trial at the target dose. Weigh every test pouch, repeat the trial at different hopper levels, inspect dust and seal quality, and agree on the pass criteria before the run. Also confirm what the tabletop filler does not include: the operator may still need to present each pouch, weigh or correct doses, and seal it on separate equipment.

The central decision is whether the machine can handle your powder and workflow as they exist, not whether a screw can turn a nominal 100 g setting. Research on screw feeding and auger dosing shows that material properties, feeder geometry and process settings interact; the findings support testing the real powder rather than transferring a generic accuracy claim to a different product. [1] [2]

## Start With the Actual Spice, Not the Number on the Pouch

"Spice powder" is not one feeding material. A fine chilli powder, a coarse seasoning blend, a hygroscopic curry mix and a free-flowing salt-and-spice blend can behave differently in the same hopper. Particle size, bulk density, cohesion, moisture, oil content, electrostatic behavior and how the blend settles can change how the screw fills and releases powder. The machine's dose is therefore affected by more than its programmed rotation count.

Give the supplier a representative batch, not a substitute powder chosen because it is easy to ship. If your product contains flakes, seeds or visibly different particle sizes, send the actual formulation and describe whether it is blended before filling. Ask the supplier to record the sample identity, date, hopper condition, screw or auger used, fill setting, and any agitation or vibration setting. If a formulation or grind changes between lots, ask whether that change could require a new setup or trial.

Ask how the seller defines the 100 g figure. Does it mean an adjustable dose range, a nominal volume converted using a reference powder, or an achieved net weight on a particular test material? A volumetric auger moves a repeatable screw volume; the mass of that volume depends on how the product packs. Do not treat an on-screen setpoint as a verified net weight.

One published study examined ten pharmaceutical powders, three auger geometries and three screw speeds and found that material attributes and process settings mattered to dosing behavior. Another study linked powder flow and density properties to screw-feeder performance. [1] [2] Those studies are not spice-machine acceptance tests, and they do not predict a particular tabletop machine's result. They do explain why a generic "powder" demonstration is weak evidence for your blend.

## What a Listed 5-100 g Range Does and Does Not Prove

The product listing supplied for this article describes a tabletop dry-powder auger filler in 5 g, 10 g, 30 g, 50 g and 100 g variants. That identifies a plausible machine class for a small pouch operation. It does not by itself show that each setting is available on one unchanged screw, that 100 g works with every powder, or that the seller guarantees a specific tolerance at that weight. The seller's live configuration, motor, control mode and included tooling should be confirmed against the exact quotation.

For a 100 g pouch, ask for the measured results at the top of the stated range. Request the individual gross and tare weights or net weights from a trial, not only the average and not only a video showing the machine cycling. An average can hide alternating light and heavy fills. Ask what scale was used, its resolution and calibration status, and whether the operator corrected doses by hand after the filler discharged.

Agree on a target mean and an allowable range for individual pouches before the test. The right limits depend on the product, package claim and sales market; there is no universal tolerance that can be inferred from a marketplace listing. Record the agreed test method and decide who is responsible for determining legal net-quantity compliance in the destination market.

## Run a Trial That Looks Like Your Shift

A useful trial is designed around the likely sources of variation, rather than a supplier's best-looking demonstration. Send enough product for setup, adjustment, and repeated runs. If the powder is expensive or difficult to ship, agree on a smaller but still meaningful protocol and state what the reduced test cannot establish.

Use the actual pouch size, opening width, laminate, zipper or gusset, and intended filling position. If the planned operation fills into premade pouches by hand, demonstrate the operator's reach and pouch support. If the equipment is expected to fill directly into a pouch machine, test that interface instead. A tabletop dosing head that dispenses powder is not automatically a pouch-forming or heat-sealing machine.

Ask the supplier to run and document at least these conditions:

| Condition | What to observe |
| --- | --- |
| Start-up after loading | First fills after the hopper is charged; note whether the operator discards or adjusts initial doses. |
| Steady run | Individual net weights across a continuous run, with the agreed auger, speed and operator method. |
| Lower hopper level | Whether dose behavior changes as the powder head and hopper contents fall. |
| After a refill | Whether adding product causes settling, aeration, bridging or a temporary change in fills. |
| Pouch handling | Powder on the seal zone, pouch stability, dust, spill rate and any manual correction or rework. |

Ask for the raw observations and video together. Video can confirm the tested configuration and show the operator's actions; it cannot replace the weights. Request a video that includes the machine nameplate or quoted model, the auger or tooling, the actual powder, and enough of the run to see whether fills were adjusted manually. Keep the sample and setup record if you later compare another machine.

Set acceptance criteria before testing. For example, specify the required proportion of pouches inside your own agreed weight band, the maximum rate of rejects or corrections, the pouch-seal cleanliness requirement and the output you need after weighing and sealing. The values should come from your operation and market obligations, not be invented by the seller or copied from a different powder.

## Find Out Whether the Dose Is Volumetric or Weight-Controlled

An auger can be controlled by screw turns or run time, by a weighing system, or by a combination that first doses and then trims to a target. These are different control arrangements. Ask the seller to identify which one is in the exact quotation and where the weight is measured. A display that shows a target number is not proof that the machine weighs every pouch or corrects every dose.

If the offer includes a load cell or weighing feedback, ask to see it working during the powder trial. Confirm whether the pouch or a receiving cup is weighed, how tare is handled, whether feedback changes the auger motion, and what happens when the powder bridges or the target is missed. Request the measured results with feedback enabled and disabled if both modes are offered. For an operator-corrected volumetric system, include that correction time in the output calculation.

The published auger-dosing and screw-feeding studies relate powder attributes and feeder setup to measured behavior; they do not establish that a small tabletop machine has gravimetric correction. [1] [2] Verify the controls in the quoted machine rather than assuming that "precision," "high accuracy" or a digital panel describes a feedback loop.

## Check the Pouch and the Seal Zone

A tabletop filler may meter powder accurately and still make a poor pouch if the discharge is dusty or the pouch opening is awkward to hold under the nozzle. Show the supplier the pouch drawing or physical sample. Confirm the opening width and height, whether it has a zipper, gusset or spout, where the seal begins, and how the operator will keep the pouch square during filling.

During the trial, inspect the inside and outside of the seal area before closing the pouch. Powder caught in a heat-seal zone can interfere with a clean closure. Ask the supplier to demonstrate the intended sequence: hold the pouch, position the nozzle, dispense, withdraw the nozzle, check the lip, then seal. If the supplier uses a collar, nozzle extension or dust hood, confirm whether it is included and whether it suits your pouch opening.

Measure output as accepted, sealed pouches per hour, not only auger cycles or fills per minute. Include time for loading a pouch, starting the fill, checking weight, correcting an underfill if necessary, wiping or clearing the pouch mouth, sealing, and moving the finished pouch. A headline cycle rate omits these labor steps unless the supplier defines it otherwise.

## Ask How the Product-Contact Parts Are Cleaned

Spice changes create a practical cleaning question: how quickly can the product-contact path be emptied, opened, cleaned and reassembled without leaving residues in corners or around the auger? Request a parts diagram and a demonstration of disassembly. Ask which parts are removable without tools, which surfaces contact product, what gaskets or seals are present, and which cleaning methods the manufacturer permits.

If the machine will handle food, request written material documentation for the product-contact components and the intended cleaning regime. A listing statement such as "stainless steel" or "304/316" does not identify every contact surface or establish suitability for every food, cleaning chemical or jurisdiction. Confirm the exact grade, finish, welds, seals and any coatings in the offered configuration. Do not infer food-contact compliance from a broad catalog label.

For allergen or strong-aroma changeovers, ask what cleaning evidence the supplier can provide and define your own acceptance check. A supplier's ability to remove parts does not prove that your cleaning procedure prevents carryover. If you run one spice continuously, cleanability may matter mainly for planned maintenance; if you alternate products, it can drive labor and downtime.

## Confirm the Operator, Utilities and Service Plan

A tabletop footprint does not mean the machine is plug-and-play. Confirm the working height, overall footprint, shipped weight, electrical voltage and phase, frequency, plug, power draw, grounding requirements, and whether compressed air is required for the quoted configuration. Check the available bench, clearance above the hopper, access for cleaning, and whether the pouch sealer fits beside the filler.

Clarify what is included in the price: auger, hopper, foot pedal, platform or pouch holder, weighing feedback, spare parts, installation, operating manual, training and remote support. Ask how settings are saved and how the operator returns to an approved setup after cleaning. For repeat orders, request the model code and a written list of configuration changes that would require a new trial.

For support, ask who troubleshoots the screw drive, controls and weighing components; what languages the manual and service calls support; which wear parts are stocked; and how long the supplier expects replacement parts to take. These answers matter more when one filler is the only route for a product than when it is a backup or pilot tool.

## Compare Cost per Accepted Pouch

A lower machine price can still produce a higher operating cost if an operator has to reweigh and correct many doses, clear dust from the seal area, or wait for cleaning and changeover. Estimate the full task: setup, loading, filling, weighing, correction, sealing, inspection and cleanup. Compare offers using accepted sealed pouches per operator-hour, not the maximum cycle rate in a listing.

For a small run, calculate the cost of expected rejects and rework using your own trial data. Keep machine purchase, freight, duty, spare parts, pouch sealer, scale and training visible as separate line items. Do not treat marketplace prices as landed cost until the seller confirms configuration, shipping terms and destination charges in writing. If the supplier will not provide enough information for that comparison, leave the offer as a lead and continue the trial process rather than selecting by headline price.

## Decide Whether a Tabletop Filler Is Enough

A semi-automatic tabletop filler can fit a small-batch workflow when an operator can present pouches one at a time and a separate sealer is acceptable. It may be a poor fit if the required accepted output depends on continuous pouch feeding, automatic weight correction, dust containment or tightly controlled labor. Ask for a complete process proposal if the buyer needs integrated pouch forming, filling and sealing.

The comparison should be made on the same powder and package, not on machine labels. Ask each supplier to quote the same target dose, pouch, output basis, accuracy method, cleaning expectations and utilities. A more automated line may reduce handoffs but add setup, footprint and service needs; a tabletop unit may cost less to integrate but leave weighing and sealing to the operator. The correct choice is the one that meets the acceptance criteria across a real run.

## A Concise RFQ for a 100 g Spice-Pouch Trial

Send the supplier a short, complete brief. It should let the supplier choose the auger and test setup without guessing what "100 g spice pouch" means:

| RFQ field | What to send |
| --- | --- |
| Product | Name the blend; note particle size range, visible flakes/seeds, oiliness or moisture sensitivity, and whether lots vary. |
| Dose | Target net weight, accepted individual range, whether the same machine must cover other weights, and who sets the acceptance rule. |
| Sample | Representative quantity, lot/date, and whether the supplier may keep or return unused powder. |
| Pouch | Material/laminate if known, width, height, gusset/zipper, opening size, seal width and a physical sample. |
| Output | Required accepted and sealed pouches per hour, operator count, and whether the process is manual or integrated. |
| Trial evidence | Individual fill weights, scale information, run conditions, hopper-level/refill checks, pouch photos/video, rejects and manual corrections. |
| Machine | Exact model, auger and nozzle, control mode, contact-part material documents, utilities, included parts and warranty/service scope. |
