> **Quick answer.** A band sealer is a sensible upgrade when the sealing step is the measured bottleneck and your pouch format runs predictably enough to feed through it. It is not automatically better because it is called continuous. A foot-pedal impulse sealer can remain the better fit for short, mixed runs, while a band sealer can raise sustained output only if bag handling, seal quality and downstream work keep pace.

Before choosing, test the exact coffee pouch, including its zipper, gussets and laminate, then compare accepted bags per hour in a realistic run. The five checks below help a small roaster decide whether the constraint is the sealing machine, the operator's bag presentation, or another step in pack-out. This is about sealing the pouch mouth; it does not assess a one-way valve or the pouch's oxygen barrier.

## 1. Identify the Exact Pouch Structure and Seal Zone

"Coffee bag" is not a usable heat-seal specification. Roaster pouches may combine printed outer film, barrier layers, an inner sealant, a zipper, a tear notch, side seams and a bottom gusset. The outside face does not tell you which polymer forms the seal layer, whether the laminate tolerates the proposed heat exposure, or how many folded layers will meet at the top. Ask the pouch converter for the construction and the approved sealing guidance for the exact SKU. If the bag is custom or the supplier cannot provide a material sheet, say so in the machine inquiry and send physical bags.

Mark the usable seal area on a drawing. Measure the mouth width when opened flat, the distance from the zipper to the top edge, the clear film above the zipper, and any fold or side-gusset intersection that enters the jaw. A seal should land on the intended sealant surfaces without trapping the zipper profile, a gusset fold or coffee particles. A band machine may convey a pouch more consistently, but it cannot make an unsuitable seal zone disappear. Likewise, a longer impulse jaw does not mean the full jaw can be used safely on every bag.

Include the normal fill level and closure sequence in this check. If the operator fills first and seals afterward, the mouth may be dusty, creased or pulled out of square. If a valve or zipper is near the proposed seal path, maintain the package design's required clearance. The sample must represent the bag you actually buy, not a flat swatch from a different roll. [1]

## 2. Match the Machine Geometry to the Bag Opening

A foot-pedal impulse sealer brings a heated sealing bar to the stationary pouch. The operator squares the mouth, places it between the jaws, triggers the cycle and holds the material in position through sealing and any specified cooling period. The machine's bar length and usable jaw width must cover the pouch without forcing the operator to seal in overlapping sections. If a mouth is wider than the effective jaw, two passes can create a weak join or inconsistent overlap; ask the supplier to demonstrate the exact opening rather than assume that a nominal bar length equals usable width.

A horizontal continuous band sealer typically moves the pouch through heated belts or bands, with the operator presenting each mouth to the conveyor. That can make repeated positioning easier for one stable format, but bag height, top-edge stiffness, fill weight and the distance from the zipper to the mouth still matter. Confirm whether the machine is horizontal or vertical, how the bag is supported, which side faces up, and whether the mouth remains level as it enters and exits. A pouch that slumps or tilts may need a different orientation, a guide or a stand; a seller video using a small empty flat bag does not settle that question.

Request the dimensional drawing for the quoted configuration. Compare maximum bag width and height, required clearance around the sealer, working height, conveyor direction and the room needed to load and collect bags. Measure the area where the machine will sit, including space for the operator and filled-bag bins. Also confirm the exact electrical version, plug, frequency and any compressed-air or exhaust requirements. These are purchase-configuration questions, not details to infer from a model family name.

## 3. Compare Accepted Bags per Hour, Not a Speed Label

Start with a number from your own operation: how many saleable bags must leave pack-out during the hours actually available for sealing? Divide the target accepted bags by that available time to get the required rate. Then time the existing process across a representative run. Include opening the pouch, filling or receiving a filled pouch, clearing the top, aligning it, triggering the sealer, removing the bag and placing it for the next step. A quoted heater cycle omits most of that work.

Foot-pedal operation can be efficient when one person fills or stages bags while another seals, or when bag sizes and daily runs change often. It can also become tiring or inconsistent if the operator must manually align every pouch, press the pedal and wait for the heat/cool cycle before moving on. The pedal keeps hands available to hold the mouth, but it does not remove the alignment task. Track cycle time, accepted seals, rework and operator handoffs for a full batch rather than timing a few ideal cycles.

A band sealer reduces the repeated clamp-and-release motion, but its conveyor speed is not the same as finished-bag throughput. The operator still has to feed each pouch squarely and keep bags separated. A printer, checkweigher, cooling section, case-packing step or upstream filler may set the line rate. Run a pilot with the normal staff and downstream arrangement; record accepted bags per minute over a sustained interval, interruptions, jams, rejects and the time to switch between SKUs. Only then can you estimate labor per accepted bag or whether the equipment cost is justified.

Do not convert a machine's maximum belt speed into a production promise. Pouch length, space between bags, operator feed rhythm and any required cooling or inspection change the count. The useful comparison is the stable output the roastery can repeat on its real bag, with ordinary operators and the normal amount of bag variation.

If the band sealer is faster, compare the total cost per accepted bag rather than dividing its price by a headline speed. Include installation, freight, duties, replacement belts or heaters, cleaning time, operator labor, rejects and the value of capacity left unused during short runs. A change may still be worthwhile for ergonomics or repeatability, but describe that benefit separately from labor savings. Use your actual annual volume and a measured accepted rate; do not assume that all bags produced during a demonstration will be saleable.

## 4. Establish a Seal Window for the Real Laminate

Heat sealing depends on the material stack and the conditions at the seal interface. Temperature, contact time, pressure, jaw condition and cooling can interact; changing one setting may improve one defect while creating another. With impulse equipment, the control may be a timed current pulse rather than a displayed jaw temperature. With a band sealer, the machine may let you adjust temperature, belt speed, pressure or belt height. Ask the seller which variables are adjustable on the exact model and how the operator confirms that settings have stabilized. Do not compare a temperature number from one machine with a timer value from another as if they were equivalent.

The practical goal is a repeatable operating window, not one attractive sample. Test multiple settings on the same pouch construction and inspect seals made at the beginning and later in the run. Look for channels, wrinkles, incomplete edge contact, scorching, film thinning, distortion or laminate separation. A seal can look closed while still having an unbonded path; visual checks are useful but do not establish seal strength or leak integrity by themselves. Keep the top clean and flat, since coffee dust or a crease across the seal can defeat an otherwise reasonable setting.

ASTM F2029 describes laboratory preparation of heat seals for studying sealability, including hot-bar or impulse methods. It explicitly treats laboratory results as a starting relationship: differences in equipment scale, seal area and processing speed can make production-machine output different, and it does not supply production-equipment validation. [2] For a roaster, this means a material supplier's seal curve can guide a trial, but the actual machine still needs a sample run using the production bag and operating sequence.

## 5. Prove the Result Through the Whole Packing Step

Write the acceptance criteria before the trial. At minimum, define what counts as an incomplete seal, what degree of cosmetic marking is acceptable, how the package should open, and what package-level checks matter for your product and route. Use a consistent sample plan that covers different points in the run and, where practical, more than one bag lot. Record the pouch SKU or revision, fill condition, machine model, setting, operator, date and result. If the supplier changes the pouch, heater, belt, fixture or software after the trial, treat that as a configuration change and repeat the relevant checks.

For quantitative seal strength, ASTM F88/F88M measures the force required to separate a test strip that contains the seal and records the failure mode. It can support process validation and control when the buyer defines a suitable method and acceptance limit. It does not alone show that every seal is continuous, that a finished package is leak-free, or that the full coffee bag retains its intended barrier performance. [3] Pair strength measurements, when warranted, with inspection and a buyer-selected package integrity check suited to the failure risk. Do not use a supplier's "passed" statement without the sample configuration, test method, sample count and acceptance rule.

Run filled pouches, not only empty film, if filling changes how the mouth is handled. Include normal cleaning and changeover steps. Check that the operator can keep the seal zone free of product, that the machine does not distort the zipper or leave sharp folds, and that sealed bags can move into coding, weighing, cartons or storage without being reopened or crushed. Repeat at the actual speed you intend to use. A successful five-bag demonstration proves only that those five samples sealed under those conditions.

Keep the signed-off sample and settings with the pouch specification. Train operators to reject a bag when the top is contaminated or outside the qualified setup rather than stretching cycle time or temperature until it looks acceptable. Recheck after a material, pouch supplier, machine part, set point or throughput change. That small record is more useful than remembering that a machine once worked on "similar coffee bags."

## Which Machine Should You Trial First?

| Observed situation | Trial focus | What would justify the change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short batches, changing bag sizes | Foot impulse sealer with correct jaw length and operator method | Existing method makes acceptable seals without holding up filling or pack-out |
| Longer repeat runs, one steady bag format | Horizontal band sealer on filled production bags | Sustained accepted-bag rate improves after rejects, feed handling and downstream steps are included |
| Seal contamination, folds or uncertain laminate | Resolve bag and seal-zone conditions first | Do not treat more speed or heat as a fix for an unresolved package mismatch |

Keep the impulse sealer in service when the current setup passes your bag checks, runs are short or mixed, and sealing is not the constraint limiting accepted output. Consider a band sealer when a stable pouch format runs often, repeated jaw handling is the bottleneck, and the supplier can demonstrate the required sustained output on your filled bag. If neither machine meets the material, geometry or repeatability criteria, revisit the pouch or seal-zone design before buying a faster machine.

## Questions to Put in the RFQ

Send the supplier your pouch drawing, construction or converter specification, filled sample and target accepted-bag rate. Ask for written answers to these points:

- Which exact model, revision, electrical configuration and jaw or belt assembly are included in the quote?
- What bag dimensions, seal width and laminate construction were used in the demonstration, and what conditions were recorded?
- Can the seller run our pouch at our intended fill and speed, then return samples and a continuous-run video?
- Which controls are adjustable, how are settings monitored, and what start-up or warm-up procedure is required?
- What routine wear parts, cleaning steps, safety guards, spare parts, warranty terms and technical documents come with this configuration?
- For a band sealer, what does the quoted speed measure, and what sustained accepted-bag count did the sample trial produce?
- For a pedal sealer, what is the usable jaw length and the recommended sealing and cooling sequence for our pouch?

A useful quote includes the exact configuration and a sample result. "Suitable for coffee bags" is a starting claim, not a compatibility record.
