> **Quick answer.** A small roaster selling 250 g retail bags usually does not need an in-line checkweigher simply because the label says 250 g. The first requirement is a dependable way to control the coffee dose and verify that sealed packs contain the declared net quantity. A suitable bench scale, a repeatable weighing method and a clear response when results drift may be enough for a hand-filled or low-speed packing bench. An in-line checkweigher becomes useful when the business needs to inspect every moving pack, automatically remove packs outside a chosen band, or create production records without asking an operator to lift and weigh bags one by one.

That distinction matters because the two devices solve different problems. A bench scale measures a stationary bag. A dynamic checkweigher measures a bag while it moves through a conveyor and may signal or reject a pack, depending on the supplied configuration. Neither device, by itself, guarantees correct net contents. The operator still needs the right target, a scale suitable for the intended use, a stable process and a procedure for handling results that fail the shop's limits.

This guide is for small and growing roasters packing finished whole-bean or ground coffee into sealed retail pouches, with a worked focus on 250 g packs. It is not a legal compliance procedure. Net-quantity rules and legal-metrology requirements vary by market. NIST Handbook 133 is a United States reference for package-content testing; use the authority and rules that apply where the coffee is sold. [E1][E2]

## A Sealed Bag Reading Is Gross Weight, Not Net Coffee

A scale reading from a closed pouch is its gross weight: coffee plus every part of the package included on the platform. The declared net quantity is the coffee alone. In a simplified calculation, package net weight equals gross weight minus tare. Tare can include the pouch, valve, zipper, label, tie, or other packaging components, depending on the pack and the applicable test method. NIST Handbook 133 describes net-contents testing procedures that account for package tare; its formal methods include sampling and defined test steps, not merely weighing one finished bag on a convenient kitchen scale. [E1][E3]

For an internal production check, gross-weight measurement can be operationally useful if the roaster has established the tare for the exact packaging configuration and controls changes to it. It is not safe to assume every pouch weighs the same. A printed label, an added valve, a different film structure, a zipper, or a supplier change can shift package weight. If the production target is a 250 g net fill and the tare assumption is wrong by several grams, the displayed gross-weight target can be wrong by the same amount.

A practical control is to weigh a representative set of empty packaging components from each current packaging lot, using the method appropriate to the business's risk and local requirements. Record the component configuration and lot, calculate or verify a gross target for internal checks, and repeat the tare check after a packaging change. If the business cannot establish tare reliably, checking the coffee dose before the pouch is closed is a clearer process-control step than treating an unknown gross weight as proof of net contents.

Customer reports show why the distinction is easy to miss. In a coffee forum thread, a buyer questioned whether a 250 g bag included the pouch; another participant described comparing a filled bag's gross weight with a measured bag tare. In a separate roaster discussion, a customer described weighing bags and a participant mentioned random cross-checks on a second scale. These posts are anecdotes, not evidence about typical roaster performance or a legal test result. They do, however, illustrate the buyer question: does the package contain the stated coffee weight, or does the package weight get counted? [E4][E8]

## What a Bench Scale Can and Cannot Tell You

A bench scale is a useful quality-control instrument when the workflow is still manual. An operator can check the first packs after setup, inspect samples during a run, investigate a complaint, or compare the output of a filler with a reference measurement. A checkweighing model may offer target bands, audible or visual alerts, accumulation, or data output. Basic bench scales may only display mass. Do not assume an alarm, a saved record, automatic rejection, or a particular legal approval from the word "scale" in a listing; verify each function on the exact model.

Sampling is not the same as checking every unit. If only selected bags are weighed, a short-lived dosing drift can affect several unmeasured packs between checks. The extent of that risk depends on how often the operator samples, how quickly a problem develops, whether the filling process is stable, and what happens when a result is outside the internal limit. A written response should say whether to stop, adjust, hold product since the last acceptable check, reweigh it, and document release. The right interval is process-specific. This guide does not set a universal sample frequency.

A readable display increment is also not the same thing as measurement accuracy. A listing that says "1 g" may refer to display division; it does not establish error across the working range, repeatability with flexible coffee bags, calibration status, or legal-for-trade suitability. Ask for the specification and test method. Then check the scale with traceable or otherwise appropriate test weights at a point near the 250 g working load, follow the manufacturer's procedure, and keep the scale level and free from vibration or drafts that affect the reading. NIST Handbook 44 is a United States reference for weighing-device specifications and tolerances in commercial applications; local approval requirements should be checked in the destination market. [E2][E5]

A static scale is usually easier to fit into a small packing bench than a conveyor system, but ergonomics still matter. The bag must sit fully on the pan without touching a wall or operator's hand. The tare function must behave as expected, the pan must accommodate the pouch, and the display should be visible while the operator handles warm or freshly packed product. A scale that is technically capable but awkward to use can produce skipped checks and inconsistent handling.

## When an In-Line Checkweigher Earns Its Place

A dynamic checkweigher is designed to weigh packs in motion. A typical system has an infeed, a weighing conveyor, an outfeed and a controller; some models include a reject device and record results. Manufacturer guidance distinguishes this from static weighing, which is used for manual checks or spot sampling. The precise functions vary by equipment configuration. A conveyor without a suitable reject mechanism does not automatically remove an underweight bag, and a weight reading alone does not prove that coffee mass was measured independently of packaging tare. [E6]

The strongest case for in-line inspection is operational. It may fit when the line produces packs faster than people can make reliable manual checks, when the buyer requires inspection of every pack, when the cost of repeatedly handling sealed bags is material, or when automatic rejection and traceable records are part of the required process. The decision should come from the roaster's actual line pace and failure cost, not a supplier's generic statement that automation saves labor or giveaway.

A high-volume coffee case study from a checkweigher vendor describes a facility running 250 g and 500 g packs at more than 18,000 bags per shift and replacing random manual checks with in-line inspection. That is useful as a contrast in scale, but it is a supplier-reported example, not a threshold for small roasters and not independent proof of savings. A handful of short runs with frequent SKU changes may have different economics from a long, steady run even if the total weekly bag count looks similar. [E7]

Dynamic weighing also introduces integration requirements. Pouches must transfer onto and off the weigh belt consistently. A flexible bag that lands skewed, rocks, rubs a guide, or changes position may challenge repeatable measurement. The buyer needs to test the actual pouch, fill, seal, product, conveyor speed and reject timing together. A catalogue accuracy figure is not a substitute for a witnessed acceptance test on representative production packs. Ask whether the stated figure is a typical or guaranteed result, how it was measured, at what speed and load, and what the vendor counts as an acceptable false-reject rate.

## Use One Shift of Evidence to Decide

Before requesting quotations, run a short, documented review on the current packing process. The purpose is not to declare regulatory compliance. It is to learn whether the bottleneck is dosing, verification, labor, rework, or recordkeeping, and whether a static or dynamic device addresses it.

First, write down the pack variants that matter: 250 g whole bean or ground coffee, pouch style, valve and zipper, label configuration, and any other sale sizes on the same line. Record whether the operator controls fill weight before sealing, checks a sealed gross weight, or does both. List current scales and note their capacity, display division, calibration or verification status, and any required market approval that has been confirmed. Do not treat a seller's display-resolution claim as evidence of accuracy.

Next, observe a representative shift or a representative run of each common SKU. Record the time and count of packed bags; the fill or gross-weight results that operators already collect; the number of checks skipped or delayed; and the minutes spent weighing, opening, reworking or holding packs. Log start-up, changeover and interruptions separately from steady running. If results vary, note whether the variation follows a particular operator, coffee format, filler setting, pouch type or time since the last adjustment. Avoid inventing a minimum number of bags: choose observations that reflect the actual run and are sufficient for the roaster's own risk review.

Then define the control action. What result triggers an adjustment? Who may release held bags? How far back does the hold extend after a failed sample? Is the target expressed as net fill or as gross bag weight with verified tare? A scale can expose variation, but only a defined reaction prevents an alert from becoming a note that no one acts on. For regulatory decisions, use the applicable official sampling method and qualified advice rather than converting a one-shift observation into a compliance conclusion. [E1][E3]

Finally, compare the measured manual burden and the frequency of problems with the cost and space of a dynamic unit. Include operator time, product rework, potential giveaway, changeover effort, floor space, compressed air or power if required, integration, service, spare parts, training and data retention. A checkweigher does not fix an unstable upstream filler. If the underlying dose fluctuates widely, the filler may need adjustment before adding downstream equipment.

## A Supplier Trial Should Use Real Coffee Pouches

For a bench scale, bring sealed 250 g pouches from the actual process and ask the supplier to demonstrate tare handling, repeatability, target alerts, units, data output and operation at the intended working load. Test more than one pouch orientation. Confirm the platform size and capacity leave adequate room for the pack without making the weighing setup cumbersome. Ask what calibration, verification, repair and local service are available, and whether the exact model is approved for the intended commercial use in the destination market. Request the model's manual and complete specifications rather than relying on a listing headline.

For an in-line unit, request a trial with representative whole-bean and ground-coffee bags if both will run on the system. Specify pouch dimensions, finished pack weight, closure type, planned conveyor speed, spacing between packs, reject method and the expected range of SKU changeovers. Ask the supplier to show a written test plan and results that separate stated capacity, nominal line speed, weighing accuracy, system repeatability, false rejection, missed rejection, and net-versus-gross handling. Confirm whether each bag receives a unique recorded result and whether the record can be exported in a usable format.

The acceptance test should include known under-target and over-target conditions at the limits that the roaster actually intends to control, without presenting those internal limits as legal tolerances. Observe reject timing and confirm that a rejected bag cannot quietly return to good product. Check safe access for clearing a jam and whether an operator can change recipes without losing the audit trail. Put agreed trial conditions and acceptance criteria in the purchase specification. A video of an empty conveyor or a generic demonstration pack does not answer whether the roaster's coffee pouch will transfer and weigh consistently.
