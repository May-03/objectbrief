> **Quick answer.** Do not trust the capacity on the listing for your net weight. Suppliers list six-cavity clamshells at 2.4, 2.5, 2.7, 2.75 and even 3.5 ounces, and one supplier's own pages give two different figures for similar clamshells. Fill, pour temperature and wax type change the real number. Pour at the temperature the clamshell maker states, usually around 140 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit, because hotter wax can warp the plastic. Put a warning label and a primary label on the pack, and weigh a sample of finished clamshells to set the declared weight. In the UK and EU, labelling rules for fragranced melts are more detailed; the summary below follows what the guidance says and do not give legal advice.

## Capacity: the number on the listing is a ceiling

CandleScience says its standard six-piece clamshell holds 2.75 ounces (78 grams) of wax, with melts about one inch on a side, and calls it the most common size in the market [1]. Hive and Honey Candle Supply lists a similar six-cavity clamshell at about 2.7 ounces filled to the edge and 3 ounces filled to the top [2]. Midwest Fragrance Company's product page for its six-cavity "improved version" gives a maximum of 2.7 ounces [3], yet its help page says the six-cavity clamshell holds approximately 2.4 ounces [4]. Bulk Apothecary and a Walmart marketplace listing both state 2.4 ounces [5][6]. Nature's Garden lists a clamshell at approximately 3.5 ounces for six one-inch snaps [7], and a wholesale listing on Faire gives 2.5 ounces with 1 inch cubes [8].

|Listing|Stated capacity|Note|
|---|---|---|
|CandleScience standard 6 pc [1]|2.75 oz (78 g)|1 in cubes|
|Hive and Honey 6 cavity [2]|2.7 oz to edge, 3 oz to top|Edge versus top fill|
|Midwest Fragrance 6 cavity [3][4]|max 2.7 oz (product page); 2.4 oz (help page)|Two figures, one supplier|
|Bulk Apothecary 6 cavity [5]|2.4 oz|0.024 in PET|
|Nature's Garden [7]|about 3.5 oz|Six 1 in snaps|
|Faire wholesale listing [8]|2.5 oz|1 in cubes|

Supplier statements as displayed. Our arithmetic: 2.75 oz is about 78 g, matching the 78 g CandleScience gives, and 2.4 oz is about 68 g. The spread from 2.4 to 3.5 oz is about 1.1 oz, or roughly 31 g.

The spread has several plausible causes, and none is stated by the sources: different cavity depth, whether the lid pushes wax out, a pour that stops at the edge or at the top, and the density of the wax. Hive and Honey is the only one that tells you both numbers, and the gap is 0.3 ounces, about 11 percent by the arithmetic here [2]. That is the practical lesson: edge versus top fill alone can change your weight by a tenth.

Smaller formats carry the same caveat. CandleScience lists a five-piece snap-bar clamshell at 1.5 ounces (42.5 grams) for five melts and a ten-piece mini square at 1.5 ounces (43 grams) [9][10]. Voyageur Soap and Candle lists a round six-cavity pie-shaped clamshell at 2 ounces, 3.5 inches across [11].

## Pouring: temperatures and the 85/15 trick

Suppliers agree on a range, not an exact number. Bulk Apothecary recommends pouring at 140 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit with a 160 maximum [5]. Midwest Fragrance recommends 140 to 175 degrees with a 175 maximum for its improved version, and warns about pouring at high temperatures [12]. Nature's Garden says to pour at 140 degrees [7]. A recycled-PET clamshell on Faire says to cool the wax to 131 degrees before pouring [13], and one listing warns that heat can warp and deform clamshells [14]. Hive and Honey says to melt the wax at about 195 to 215 degrees and then pour at 140 to 160 [2].

|Source|Pour temperature|Note|
|---|---|---|
|Bulk Apothecary [5]|140 to 160 F|Maximum 160 F|
|Midwest Fragrance improved version [12]|140 to 175 F|Maximum 175 F|
|Hive and Honey [2]|140 to 160 F|Melt at 195 to 215 F first|
|Nature's Garden [7]|140 F||
|Faire recycled PET [13]|cool to 131 F|Different plastic grade|

Each is the clamshell seller's statement for its own product. Do not apply one seller's maximum to another seller's clamshell.

Because the figures differ by plastic grade and maker, read the temperature that comes with your clamshell, not a forum number. If you change suppliers, reset your pour temperature. Hive and Honey also suggests pouring about 85 percent of the container, letting it cool, then pouring the remaining 15 percent for smoother tops on clamshells or larger melts [2]. That is one maker's technique; it has not been tried here.

## Material and recycling claims

Bulk Apothecary and Midwest Fragrance both describe their clamshells as 0.024 inch PET [5][3]. CandleScience says its clamshell is recyclable under code 3, which it says most curbside services accept [1], and Faire's listing for another maker says its clamshells are made from recycled PET and fully recyclable [13]. Resin identification code 3 is commonly assigned to a different plastic from PET, so the CandleScience code with the other listings from these pages alone. If you intend to print a recycling claim, ask your supplier for the resin, and check what your local collection takes. Nothing here was research recycling rules.

## Labels: what has to go where

CandleScience's label guide says candles and wax melts need a primary label on the outer packaging or the product itself, that most also carry a warning label, and that the clamshell warning label is often placed inside the lid [15]. It adds that net weight must exclude the clamshell and other packaging, and that giving the total finished weight does not satisfy the net-weight requirement [15]. That matches the arithmetic problem above: the wax inside is what you declare, so weigh it.

The warning text CandleScience prints on a ready-made label is short: warning, to prevent fire or injury, remove packaging before use [16]. The product states that it fits on the inside or outside cover of standard clamshells [16]. A seller's printed roll of 600 candle warning stickers is listed at 1.8 by 1.5 inches in waterproof gloss paper [17].

Label size is set by the clamshell: Midwest Fragrance gives front label space of 3.375 by 2.5 inches on its 4.375 by 2.9 by 1 inch closed clamshell [3], and Hive and Honey gives 3 3/8 by 2 3/8 inches on its snap-bar clamshell [18]. A buyer on The Flaming Candle's page asks whether Avery 2 1/3 by 3 3/8 inch labels fit [19]. Avery's own advice is to use waterproof, oil-resistant labels because wax melts can be oily or soft and stored near heat [20].

For the UK and EU, the picture is stricter and varies. The Irish Health and Safety Authority and French INERIS guidance both say a candle in an open container must be supplied in packaging that complies with CLP, with the label firmly affixed to the packaging surface [21][22]. Nikura, a supplier, says melts containing hazardous fragrance or essential oils are classified as non-cosmetic products needing a CLP label with a product identifier and allergen information [23]. Candle Shack notes its CLP templates add candle-safety pictograms beyond CLP requirements [24]. We are not advisers; take the CLP rules from your local trading standards or the regulator, not from this paragraph.

## Which format for which job

Format changes both the retail story and the per-pack weight. A standard six-cavity clamshell is what most buyers recognise, and CandleScience calls it the most common size [1]. The five-piece snap bar is a smaller, cheaper-to-sample pack at 1.5 ounces [9], and the ten-piece mini square at the same 1.5 ounces gives a customer more scent choices per pack because each piece is smaller [10]. Hive and Honey positions its snap bar for testing new fragrances, sampler packs and selling small quantities without committing to a full six-cavity bar [18]. That makes the small formats a sensible first run for a new scent, before you commit to full clamshells.

Shelf prices give a rough outer limit for what customers will pay. Walmart's marketplace page lists Better Homes and Gardens scented wax melts at 2.5 ounces for $1.97, shown as 78.8 cents an ounce, and another brand's melts at $5.31, shown as $2.12 an ounce [6]. Those are mass-market prices on one day and not a benchmark for a handmade product, but they show how wide the range is. Our arithmetic: a 2.5 ounce pack at the lower price carries a wax-and-package cost envelope far smaller than a small maker's, so most handmade sellers compete on fragrance and presentation rather than price. That is the reading here, not a sourced claim.

Presentation is where the clamshell earns its keep. Faire's listing for one clamshell mentions a pre-cut hanger for retail display and a lid that snaps shut and opens easily [8], and the Flaming Candle's customers say they like that bottoms come separate from lids, so many can sit on a table while pouring [28]. When you test samples, check the lid snap and the hanger hole, because those are the features that show in a shop.

## Heat in transit and sweating

Melts are the first casualty of a hot truck. ParcelPath, a shipping advice site, gives soy melts melting points of 120 to 140 degrees Fahrenheit, paraffin 130 to 140 and beeswax 145 to 155, and says delivery truck interiors can reach 120 to 140 in summer [25]. Those are a seller-side blog's figures and not a test. Ship.com says wax melts are not hazmat for shipping, and suggests dispatching early in the week and choosing faster services in summer [26]. Our hot-weather shipping page covers the logistics in more detail.

Fragrance can also show on the surface of the wax. A British candle trade body's 2024 advice notes sweating is related to fragrance content, volatility and storage, and shows as a thin film of fragrance on the wax surface [27]. In a clamshell that film can fog the inside of the lid. Nothing here was find a source on how much; try your own blend with a shelf test.

## A bench test for a new clamshell

- Buy a sample of ten clamshells from each supplier you are considering.

- Weigh five empty, then pour your usual wax to the edge and weigh them again; repeat for the top fill. Write the average, the highest and the lowest.

- Declare a net weight no higher than the lowest weight in your sample, rounded down. This is an untested rule of thumb.

- Pour at the supplier's temperature with one batch, and at 15 degrees Fahrenheit above it with another. Look for warped lids, cloudy walls or a lid that no longer snaps shut.

- Stick your chosen label on the lid and on the back and leave a pack in a warm room for a week to see whether the adhesive creeps or oil marks appear.

- Snap and unsnap each lid ten times and drop a boxed pack from table height to see whether the hinge holds.

Nothing here was run these steps. The 15 degree overshoot is a stress test devised here and not a manufacturer recommendation, so do not use it on production stock.

## Questions to ask the supplier

- What is the exact capacity at edge fill and at top fill, in ounces and grams?

- What is the plastic (resin and gauge) and the maximum pour temperature?

- What are the closed dimensions and the label space on the lid?

- Is the clamshell recyclable, under which resin code, and where does that claim come from?

- Can I buy a sample pack of ten before a case of hundreds?

## Common mistakes

- Declaring the listing capacity as net weight. Capacities vary across suppliers and with fill [1][2][3][4].

- Pouring too hot. Some listings warn heat can warp the clamshell [14].

- Counting the clamshell in the net weight. The label guidance says to exclude it [15].

- Using paper labels on oily wax. Avery advises oil-resistant labels [20].

- Skipping the warning label. Most candles and melts carry one [15][16].

## FAQ

### How much wax fits in a six-cavity wax melt clamshell?

Suppliers list 2.4 to 3.5 ounces; CandleScience says 2.75 ounces [1][2][5][7]. Weigh your own.

### What temperature should I pour wax melts at?

Clamshell sellers give 131 to 175 degrees Fahrenheit depending on the plastic; the common range is 140 to 160 [2][5][12][13].

### Do wax melt clamshells need a warning label?

Guidance from CandleScience and Avery says yes, usually inside the lid [15][16][20].

### Can I ship wax melts in summer?

Soy melts soften at 120 to 140 degrees Fahrenheit per one shipping site [25]; consider early-week dispatch and insulation [26].

### Should the wax melt pack say remove packaging before use?

The ready-made warning label from CandleScience says exactly that, to prevent fire or injury [16], and CandleScience says such warnings usually sit inside the lid [15]. Check what your own market requires, and test that the printed text survives contact with warm, oily wax.
