> **Quick answer.** The honest answer is that it depends on which product, which country and how much risk you accept. Vanish's own South African page describes its Oxi Advance Multi Power Gel as safe enough for delicate wool, with about 2 ml on the stain for no more than 10 minutes, while the wool industry body Woolmark says never to use bleach of any kind on wool, chlorine or oxygen based, and OxiClean's US pages say not to use its products on wool. Cashmere brands mostly side with caution. The reading here: start with cold water, a wool-safe detergent and blotting, treat Vanish as a last-resort option that you patch test on an inside seam first, and take set-in stains or expensive pieces to a professional cleaner. ## What Vanish itself says

Vanish's South African site says its Oxi Advance Multi Power Gel is safe enough to use on delicate items like wool, and recommends pre-treating by massaging about 2 ml of gel onto the stain, leaving it for no more than 10 minutes and then washing as usual or rinsing thoroughly [1]. It tells readers to check colour fastness on a hidden area first and to follow the packaging and the garment's care advice, with no wool-specific warnings on that page [1].

A UK grocery listing for Vanish Oxi Action gel says it can be used on delicates such as silk and wool, and the same listing carries the warning not to use it on garments requiring dry cleaning or on leather [2]. Vanish products differ between countries, so a statement on a South African page or a UK listing does not automatically apply to the bottle you own. The name and the "chlorine bleach free" wording suggest an oxygen-based booster, but nothing here was retrieve an ingredient list, so we do not say what is in it.

Cashmere is a fine animal hair, and the Vanish pages the sources read talk about wool, not cashmere by name. That is the first gap: the manufacturer's claim covers wool; applying it to cashmere is your inference.

## What wool and cashmere sources say

Woolmark, the wool industry's care authority, advises against heavy-duty detergents, biological detergents containing enzymes and any detergent with bleach for washing wool [3], and in its detergent guidance says never to use bleach, whether chlorine or oxygen based [4]. That sits uncomfortably with a product named for oxygen action. Woolmark's own stain pages are more nuanced: for some stains they suggest dabbing with a dilute wool detergent in tepid water, then a rubbing-alcohol mix, and for tougher marks a teaspoon of biological detergent in a cup of warm water for 15 minutes, followed by a rinse and a diluted white vinegar dab to neutralise it [5]. That is the industry body itself suggesting a limited, rinsed use of an enzyme product on a spot, which is worth remembering when someone says enzymes always ruin wool.

The Martha Stewart Living cashmere guide says to avoid enzyme-based cleaners because they can break down protein fibres like cashmere over time, and to never use chlorine bleach, oxygen bleach (sodium percarbonate) or baking soda [6]. Veronica Beard's care guide tells readers to blot from the outside in, never rub, and to skip bleach and OxiClean [7]. The Laundress says to pre-treat with a stain remover made for woolens and knits, test in an inconspicuous spot, use cool or tepid water and not soak for more than 30 minutes [8]. Eileen Fisher suggests cold running water for fresh stains, and for set-in stains a wool-safe stain remover, a 10 minute cold soak and a normal hand wash [9].

Mr Porter's cashmere care guide puts the same point in one sentence: you cannot just throw cashmere into the drum with a scoop of Vanish [10]. Two enzyme-based brands go further. Zero Co says its laundry liquid and stain remover are not recommended for silk, wool or cashmere because the enzymes could slightly damage protein fibres over time [11], and Koh says not to use its stain remover on wool, silk, cashmere or alpaca, because those fibres are sensitive to enzymes and may weaken, fuzz or lose colour [12].

## Why the sources disagree, and how we read it

Three things drive the disagreement. First, product formulas differ between brands and countries, so a safe statement for one gel says nothing about another. Second, wool authorities and cashmere sellers give advice written for the worst case, a customer who soaks, rubs and walks away, while a manufacturer's page describes a controlled use: 2 ml, 10 minutes, rinse. Third, the sources are not equal. Woolmark and the product makers are primary sources for their own claims, while the lifestyle articles repeat a rule of thumb.

The reading here, which is judgement and not a measurement: if the garment is cheap or the stain is small, a controlled, tested use of a wool-safe product from the maker is a reasonable risk; if the garment is expensive or the stain is old, the cautious route in the next section is better. The harm from getting it wrong is thinning, fuzzing or colour loss, which is permanent, while a professional cleaner costs a known amount.

## A cautious order of operations

This ladder is a synthesis of the sources above. Nothing here was test it. It moves from the least to the most risky step; stop as soon as the stain is gone.

- Blot, do not rub. Lift any solid or liquid with a white tissue or absorbent towel, working from the edge of the stain inward, as Woolmark and Veronica Beard advise [5][7].

- Rinse fresh stains under cold running water until the mark lifts, as Eileen Fisher suggests [9]. Keep the water cool or tepid; The Laundress says to use cool or tepid water when treating wool [8].

- If a mark remains, dab with a cloth soaked in a dilute solution of wool detergent in tepid water, from the edge inward, then blot [5]. Use a wool and cashmere shampoo or a Woolmark-approved detergent, not a heavy-duty or biological one [3].

- Test any remover on an inside seam first. Koh's checklist, written for its own product, is to apply to a hidden seam, wait 5 to 10 minutes, rinse and check; Vanish's page also advises a colour-fastness check on a hidden area [1][12].

- Only if the stain persists and you have decided to use a Vanish gel on a garment you can accept the risk on: use the gel the maker names for wool, about 2 ml on the stain, no more than 10 minutes, then rinse thoroughly [1]. Do not put it on a garment labelled dry clean only [2].

- Rinse out every trace, hand wash in cool water with a wool and cashmere shampoo, and dry flat. Our pages on washing and drying cashmere explain how.

- If the stain is set in, large or on an expensive piece, take it to a professional cleaner and tell them what you have already used. Martha Stewart Living suggests the same when a stain persists or the fabric shows damage [6].

## How to patch test properly

Almost every source asks for a patch test and few say how, so here is a composite of the instructions the sources reviewed show. Choose a hidden spot such as an inside side seam or the hem facing, because Vanish's page says to check colour fastness on a hidden area [1] and The Laundress says to test in an inconspicuous spot, noting that heavily dyed woolens may not take well to stain removers [8]. Apply a small amount of the product as the label directs, leave it for the shorter end of the stated time, then rinse and blot dry on a white cloth. Koh's own checklist for its product is to apply to a hidden seam, wait 5 to 10 minutes, rinse and check [12]. Look for colour on the cloth, a rough or fuzzy surface where the product sat, or a change in how the knit feels.

A dark or saturated colour is where the test matters most, because dye loss is visible and permanent. Pale colours can hide a small change, which is why a test on the inside of the garment is not a guarantee about the front. It was not possible to find a source that promises the test predicts every outcome; treat it as a way to catch an obvious problem, not as proof of safety.

Machine washing afterward follows the usual cashmere rules. Mr Porter says to choose the cold-water and delicate or hand-wash setting, and one designer it quotes recommends putting the piece in a pillowcase and using the machine's spin if you want [10]. Our washing page covers the details, and the main point here is that a stain remover is not a substitute for rinsing.

## Stain types: what the wool sources specifically say

Woolmark's stain pages give specific starting points. For coffee, tea, milk and chocolate, dab from the edge of the stain with a lint-free cloth soaked in detergent diluted in tepid water, and blot excess liquid; for black coffee, rinse the stain under cold running water straight away [5]. For lipstick, makeup and shoe polish, dab from the edge with a cloth soaked in white spirit or a spot-cleaning spray [5], and for blood remove the excess with an absorbent towel, dab with undiluted white vinegar followed by cold water, and wash with a Woolmark-approved detergent [5]. Our own page on makeup stains goes into more detail for that case.

Two cautions apply to every stain type. Woolmark says to apply small quantities of liquid treatment and blot well, work from the edge of the stain toward the centre, and rinse water-based treatments out thoroughly [5]. And Woolmark warns that many internet stain tips are written for cotton or synthetics and are not all suitable for wool [14]. A baking-soda paste or a hot-water soak that works on a cotton T-shirt can leave a cashmere sweater thinner or felted.

## Sweat and old stains

The query "how to remove sweat stains from cashmere" and "how to get old stains out of cashmere" are different problems from a fresh spill. Sweat and body-oil stains build up over wears and can look yellow, and the page on yellow underarm stains covers that in detail. Old stains are where the risk-benefit changes: a gentle method may not move them, and a stronger one is harder to justify on a thin knit. The sources reviewed show no source that promises a safe home method for a long-set stain on cashmere, so for those the reading here is to compare the cost of a cleaner with the value of the garment before escalating.

## What not to do

- Do not use chlorine bleach. Woolmark says never to use bleach of any kind on wool [4]; a page quoted in a forum thread says hypochlorite will destroy wool and silk [15].

- Do not rub or scrub. Both Woolmark and Veronica Beard say to blot [5][7].

- Do not rely on an oxygen booster without a patch test. Sources disagree, and the downside is permanent [1][4][13].

- Do not use heat. The Laundress says to use cool or tepid water and keep soaking under 30 minutes [8].

- Do not use enzyme products on cashmere as a routine. Koh and Zero Co both say not to [11][12].

## Questions to ask before you buy a stain remover for knitwear

- Does the label name wool or cashmere, or only "delicates"?

- Is it enzyme-based or oxygen-based, and does the maker say which fibres it is not for?

- Is there a stated maximum contact time and a rinse instruction?

- Does it say do not use on dry clean only garments?

- Is it sold for your market? The same brand name can mean a different formula.

## FAQ

### Can I use Vanish on cashmere?

Vanish's South African page says its Oxi Advance Multi Power Gel is safe enough for wool, with a 10 minute limit [1]; Woolmark says never to use oxygen bleach on wool [4]. Patch test, or use a wool-safe alternative.

### Is OxiClean safe on cashmere?

OxiClean's US pages say not to use it on wool, wool blends, silk or dry-clean-only fabrics [13].

### What can I use on cashmere stains instead?

Cold water, a wool and cashmere shampoo, and a stain remover made for knits such as the type The Laundress and Eileen Fisher describe [8][9].

### How long can a stain remover stay on cashmere?

The Vanish page limits its wool pre-treatment to 10 minutes [1]; Eileen Fisher suggests a 10 minute cold soak for a wool-safe remover [9]; The Laundress says under 30 minutes [8].
