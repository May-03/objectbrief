> **Quick answer.** A vacuum leak tester can help a small coffee roaster find a pouch with a gross leak or an open channel in its seal. It cannot certify that every pouch is airtight, predict shelf life, or prove that a one-way degassing valve works correctly. Those are separate questions. ASTM D3078 describes a bubble-emission method for gross leaks in flexible packaging that contains headspace gas; ASTM F88 measures seal strength. A valve needs its own functional check. The useful buying question is therefore not "Does this machine test coffee bags?" but "Which defect do I need to detect, on which finished pouch, and what decision will I make from the result?"

## Start With the Failure You Are Trying to Catch

For a small roaster, leak testing is most useful when a specific failure has a cost: a visibly deflated or damaged bag, oil or aroma escaping at a seam, returns after shipping, or inconsistent seals after a change in film, operator, or sealer settings. Before shopping, inspect returned or rejected packs and sort the evidence. A crease crossing the seal, coffee trapped in the seal area, a skipped section, a puncture, and a valve defect are not interchangeable problems. A chamber tester may reveal some paths that pass through the pouch wall or seal, but it will not tell you why the path formed. A process check at the sealer may be the faster first fix if failures cluster around one jaw, shift, or setup.

Write down the pouch construction and the condition that matters: bag style, film laminate, dimensions, fill, closure geometry, valve presence and location, and whether the sample is empty or filled. Include the sealing process that produced it. This is not paperwork for its own sake. Flexible packs deform under vacuum, and product position or trapped air can alter what a test exposes. An empty film pouch is not automatically a valid stand-in for a filled coffee pouch. Ask the equipment seller which representative sample they need and whether the proposed fixture accommodates your actual bag.

## What Bubble-Emission Testing Shows

In the ASTM D3078 approach, the package is placed in a transparent chamber, commonly under liquid, and pressure is reduced around it. Gas escaping through a leak can appear as a stream of bubbles. The method is aimed at gross leaks in flexible packages that contain headspace gas, rather than at every microscopic defect or every form of package degradation. The operator needs to see the sample clearly and distinguish a repeatable bubble stream from air released from folds, trapped surface bubbles, or movement during chamber evacuation. The standard also calls for a chamber that can withstand the pressure difference and an immersion liquid that does not damage the package.

That gives the method a practical role: a roaster can compare suspect samples with acceptable ones, look for a leak path, and investigate whether the seal or pouch wall is visibly compromised under the chosen test conditions. The result is a pass/fail observation only in relation to a defined procedure and acceptance rule. "No bubbles seen" means no leak was detected under that setup; it does not mean no gas can pass through the package, that oxygen transmission is low enough for a desired shelf life, or that the package will stay intact through every distribution condition.

## Where the Result Stops Being Reassuring

ASTM cautions that small leaks may not be detected. A small opening can be blocked by product or by air entrained in the product, and sensitivity depends on the package, contents, and test parameters. That limitation matters for coffee: grounds can lodge at a seal, oil can contaminate a sealing surface, and a defect may open only when the bag is flexed or squeezed. A test on an empty pouch can miss a failure mode present in a filled pouch; a test on a filled pouch can be harder to interpret because product may obscure or plug the path. Neither result should be generalized beyond the samples and conditions actually tested.

Bubble testing also does not measure barrier performance. Oxygen and moisture can permeate through an intact laminate over time without a visible opening. A pouch can pass a gross-leak screen and still be the wrong material for a target shelf life or storage environment. Conversely, a package that loses shape during evacuation is not automatically defective: flexible packaging is expected to deform under pressure differences. The operator needs an agreed method for positioning the sample, reaching the test condition, holding it, and reading the result. Ask the vendor to demonstrate with your filled, valved pouch, not only with a simple empty bag from the showroom.

## Do Not Confuse Leakage With Weak Seals

A seam can be weak without currently leaking, and a pouch can leak through a puncture even when its seals are strong. ASTM F88/F88M is a seal-strength test for flexible barrier materials. It measures the force required to separate a cut strip of seal under a defined test arrangement. That information can help compare sealing conditions or materials, but it is destructive and does not, by itself, establish whole-package leak integrity. A bubble-emission chamber answers a different question: under its specified conditions, does gas escape from the package through a detectable path?

Small operators rarely need to buy every laboratory instrument at once. If the issue is a weak or variable seal, start with controlled seal settings and repeatable sampling, then consider sending strips to a packaging lab or using a tensile tester with a suitable fixture if you need force data. If the complaint is a gross leak in a finished pouch, a chamber method may be more direct. If the concern is oxidation or freshness over time, ask the film supplier or a qualified laboratory about oxygen transmission and package-specific shelf-life validation. A single test cannot substitute for all three.

## The Degassing Valve Changes the Test Plan

Freshly roasted coffee releases gas after packing. Many coffee pouches use a one-way degassing valve intended to let gas move outward while limiting incoming air. That valve is a designed flow path, so a bubble test on an assembled bag may produce a result that is difficult to interpret unless the equipment and procedure account for the valve. A bubble stream at the valve does not automatically mean the valve is faulty; no visible bubbles at the seam do not prove that the valve opens at the intended pressure or blocks reverse flow.

AMETEK MOCON's coffee-packaging guidance treats valve leak testing and valve release testing as different checks: one considers leakage or oxygen entry, while the other checks whether the valve opens at its intended release condition. This distinction is useful even if you use another brand of tester. Ask the manufacturer whether its D3078-style chamber is designed for coffee pouches with a valve, whether the valve should be masked or evaluated separately, and whether the proposed setup can distinguish a seam leak from normal valve behavior. Do not assume that a generic pouch fixture provides a valid valve test.

## A Practical Small-Roaster Test Routine

Begin with a written visual check at the sealing station. Confirm that the sealing area is free of grounds and oil, that the closure is complete across its width, and that there are no obvious folds, burn-through, or interrupted sections. Record the sealer setting, film lot, operator or shift, and any change made. Visual inspection will not reveal every small channel, but it helps identify process drift and gives a reference when a later chamber result is unexpected.

Next, choose samples that represent the real process. Include a normal filled pouch and any suspect format or condition you are investigating. If the objective is to catch gross leak paths, ask the vendor to run a demonstration with your own pouch and fill. Agree in advance on how the sample is prepared, how the valve is handled, what counts as a positive leak indication, and how repeat runs are treated. Keep one known-good sample and one deliberately compromised sample only if the vendor can prepare them safely and explain what the demonstration proves. A polished demonstration with a different pouch construction proves the machine operates; it does not validate your process.

Sampling should answer the question you set, not create a reassuring number without context. If you are troubleshooting a specific incident, begin with the affected pouch and a comparable pouch from the same run, then expand the set if the pattern is unclear. For routine monitoring, define the selection rule from your own risk and production volume with a packaging specialist; there is no universal sample count or interval established here for every roaster. Keep the rule stable long enough to see changes, and document any deviation. A handful of convenience samples can find a visible defect, but it cannot establish a statistically reliable defect rate.

Keep a distinction between screening and validation. A chamber check can be a useful screen for gross leaks when the method is repeatable. Validation asks whether the chosen method can detect the defect that matters in your package and whether operators reach the same conclusion. For that, ask the seller to document the pressure profile and observation criteria, test multiple representative samples, and explain the known detection limits. If the commercial or food-safety consequence is material, have a qualified packaging laboratory help design the protocol instead of treating a supplier demonstration as validation.

A useful record captures the sample identity, date, pouch and film lot, fill condition, test method, chamber settings, immersion fluid, operator, observation, and disposition. If a leak appears, isolate the pouch and inspect the location before changing the sealer. If results vary, repeat the agreed method and check sample placement, folds, trapped bubbles, seal cleanliness, and valve treatment. Do not keep adjusting settings until a preferred outcome appears. The purpose is to find a reproducible relationship between a process condition and a package result.

## What to Ask Before Buying a Tester

Ask the supplier to identify the exact method the instrument follows and whether the claim is "based on" a standard or demonstrated as compliant with a documented procedure. Request the usable chamber dimensions, supported pouch sizes, chamber material and pressure rating, vacuum control and readout, cycle steps, and the safety features that prevent operation outside the rated condition. Ask how the instrument handles liquid, cleaning, and replacement parts. For a coffee operation, confirm whether your filled pouch fits, whether the degassing valve changes the method, and whether the seller can test your samples before you order.

Also ask what the displayed result means. Does the unit simply let an operator observe bubbles, or does it provide a defined pressure profile, hold time, record, or alarm? What training is needed to separate a leak stream from residual air? What consumables and service are required? Can the seller provide the procedure, acceptance criteria, and an example report? A low purchase price is not the full cost if the chamber is too small, unsuitable for your pouch, difficult to clean, or unsupported. Conversely, an elaborate system may be excessive if you only need occasional investigation rather than routine production monitoring.

Clarify the claim language in writing. "Vacuum leak tester" is a product category description; it does not establish sensitivity, repeatability, or compliance with a named standard. Ask which revision and procedure the seller references, whether the supplied chamber and controls are within that procedure's scope, and what records accompany the unit. If a seller quotes a minimum detectable leak size, request the test conditions and validation data behind that figure. Do not compare two machines on a threshold unless the methods and sample configurations are comparable. The practical acceptance limit belongs to your package requirement, not to a marketing label.

## When This Equipment Is a Sensible Fit

A bubble-emission vacuum tester is worth evaluating when you repeatedly handle flexible pouches with headspace, need a visible gross-leak investigation, and can define a repeatable method for the exact package. It is less compelling when you have not yet established what failure you need to catch, when your main requirement is seal-force data or shelf-life prediction, or when a one-way valve makes the proposed setup uninterpretable. In those cases, first define the test question and ask a packaging lab or equipment supplier to demonstrate the correct method on your package.

For most small roasters, a sensible sequence is modest: control and document the sealing process; use visual checks to catch obvious defects; use a representative chamber demonstration when gross leaks are a real concern; and treat seal strength, barrier performance, and valve function as separate validation needs. A vacuum tester is useful when its result changes a clear decision. It is not a general-purpose certificate that a coffee pouch is sound.
