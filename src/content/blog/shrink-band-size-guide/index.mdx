> **Quick answer.** To choose a shrink band size, measure the **widest outside diameter** of the cap-and-container area the unshrunk band must pass over — not just the bottle's nominal neck size. Include flanges, curves, or other projections that increase the maximum diameter.

Shrink bands are commonly specified by **lay-flat width** and **band height**. If you measure circumference instead of diameter, the theoretical lay-flat width is half the circumference. Some suppliers publish a starting method that adds 2 mm after halving the circumference; treat that allowance as supplier-specific guidance rather than a universal rule.

Band height must be long enough to cover the closure and extend onto the bottle or jar below it. Before ordering in volume, check the supplier's stated fit range and test samples on the actual container, because the final fit depends on both the band specification and the container geometry.

## What Shrink Band Size Do You Need?

A usable shrink-band specification has two separate dimensions: how wide the unshrunk tube is when laid flat, and how tall the cut band is. Many suppliers describe these as **lay-flat width** and **band height**. The first determines whether the band can slide over the closure and then shrink tightly enough; the second determines how much of the closure and container the band can cover.

Do not assume that a listing such as "90 × 25 mm" means a 90 mm cap. One well-known supplier lists a band as 90 mm lay-flat by 25 mm high, while the stated approximate cap fit range is only 50.9–56 mm in outside diameter. That difference is normal: lay-flat width is the flattened circumference of a tubular film, not the container diameter.

Supplier notation is not perfectly standardized, so confirm which number is width and which is height before buying. The safest workflow is to match your measured container to the supplier's fit range, then confirm the band height separately.

| Supplier field | What it means | What you should verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lay-flat width | Width of the unshrunk tube when flattened | Supplier fit range for your actual outside diameter |
| Band height / cut length | Vertical coverage of the band | Enough overlap on closure and container |
| Fit range | Approximate minimum/maximum outside diameter | Your measured package falls safely inside the range |

## What Part of the Bottle or Jar Should You Measure?

### Measure the widest point the band must pass over

Measure the maximum outside diameter across the part of the closure/container assembly that the unshrunk band must slide over. Packaging suppliers typically instruct users to measure the widest part and to include flanges, curves, or flares.

This matters because a cap may have a flange, rib, shoulder, dropper-bulb base, or decorative overhang that is wider than the nominal neck. If you measure a narrower point, the band can look correct on paper but fail to slide into position.

### Do not substitute the nominal neck finish

A nominal neck or closure code is useful for matching caps to containers, but it is not a direct shrink-band measurement. Different closures associated with the same nominal neck size can require much larger lay-flat dimensions. A specialist shrink-band seller makes the same practical point with jam-jar lids: the true outside lid diameter can be several millimetres larger than the nominal lid size.

**Buyer action:** measure the actual closure you will use on the actual container. If the band has to pass over both the cap and another feature, use whichever point is widest.

## How Do You Calculate Lay-Flat Width?

### If you know the circumference

For a tubular band, the theoretical lay-flat width is **half the circumference**. If the measured circumference is 100 mm, the theoretical lay-flat is 50 mm. One published sizing method for shrink bands adds 2 mm after dividing the circumference by two, producing a 52 mm starting value in this example.

That extra 2 mm should not be treated as a universal industry constant. Different materials and suppliers may publish different fit ranges or allowances. Use the formula to identify a candidate size, then verify the actual product specification.

### If you know the maximum outside diameter

Convert diameter to circumference first: **circumference = π × diameter**. Then divide by two for the theoretical lay-flat width. For a maximum outside diameter of 33 mm, the circumference is about 103.7 mm and the theoretical lay-flat is about 51.8 mm. Applying the +2 mm starting method would give an approximate starting value of 53.8 mm.

The calculation is geometry, not a guarantee of fit. A commercial band has a defined shrink behaviour, film thickness, and manufacturing tolerance, and the supplier may give a minimum/maximum diameter range that should take priority over a generic formula.

## How Tall Should a Shrink Band Be?

Band height should cover enough of the closure and container to create the intended tamper-evident bridge: a tamper-evident band must grip both the bottle cap and the bottle neck. One supplier recommends, for its bands, allowing roughly **5 mm of overhang above and below the closure** so the film can grab the neck of the bottle or jar.

The required height therefore depends on closure height and container geometry, not only on cap diameter. A short band may shrink neatly around the cap but fail to extend far enough onto the neck. A much taller band may interfere with labels, shoulders, or decorative features.

Measure from the top of the area you want the band to cover to the lower point where the band should grip the container. Then compare that height with the supplier's available cut lengths. Remember that some of the film may fold or lip over the top edge as it shrinks — account for this when choosing between available band heights.

## Worked Examples: From Measurement to Candidate Size

### Example 1: circumference measured with a flexible tape

Suppose the maximum circumference around the closure is 100 mm. The theoretical lay-flat is 100 ÷ 2 = **50 mm**. Using the published +2 mm starting method, 100 ÷ 2 + 2 = **52 mm**. The result is a candidate starting point, not an instruction to buy any product labelled 52 mm without checking its fit range.

### Example 2: outside diameter measured with calipers

Suppose the widest outside diameter is 33 mm. Circumference is approximately 33 × 3.1416 = **103.7 mm**. The theoretical lay-flat is approximately **51.8 mm**; with the +2 mm allowance it becomes approximately **53.8 mm**. You would then look for a supplier size whose published fit range includes your 33 mm geometry after accounting for the supplier's own specification.

### Example 3: why the supplier fit range matters

One supplier lists a 90 mm lay-flat band with a published approximate outside-diameter range of 50.9–56 mm. A theoretical circular conversion of 90 mm lay-flat would correspond to a circumference of 180 mm and a diameter of about 57.3 mm — but the supplier's recommended range stops below that. This is exactly why a generic formula should be used for orientation, while the product's stated fit range and a physical sample decide the final purchase.

## How Do You Read a Supplier Fit Range or Size Chart?

### Use the fit range to confirm the formula

A formula is useful for narrowing the field, but the supplier's published fit range should control the final selection for that specific product. Suppliers that publish both a lay-flat dimension and a minimum/maximum outside-diameter range give buyers a direct way to compare a measured package with the band that is actually being sold.

The separation between width and height is visible in supplier data too: the same supplier may sell a 90 mm lay-flat × 25 mm high band and a 90 mm lay-flat × 52 mm high band with the same approximate diameter range. The width controls the circumference-related fit; changing the height changes vertical coverage without changing that published diameter range.

### What if your measurement is near the top of the range?

Do not assume the tightest possible listed fit is automatically best. Supplier guidance commonly advises choosing the **next larger size** when the measured diameter is near the top end of the band's stated fit range. The practical reason is simple: real packages have tolerances, moulded features, and measurement error. A band that only barely slips over the widest point can be difficult to position consistently.

### What if the supplier does not publish a fit range?

Ask for a sizing chart, a sample, or written confirmation against your measured maximum diameter and required band height. If the supplier only gives a generic nominal cap size, do not convert that into a confirmed fit on your own. Record the actual dimensions used for the quotation so that a later reorder can be checked against the same specification.

**Buyer action:** keep the measured outside diameter, candidate lay-flat width, supplier fit range, and sample result in the same sourcing record. That makes the final size decision traceable instead of relying on a product title or memory.

## Common Shrink Band Sizing Mistakes

- **Using nominal cap size instead of actual outside diameter.** A nominal neck or lid size is not the same thing as the widest physical dimension the band must clear. Measure the assembled package, not the catalogue label alone.
- **Measuring the narrowest convenient point.** Measure the widest diameter, including flanges, curves, and flares. If the closure has an overhang, that overhang can control the required starting width.
- **Treating lay-flat width as container diameter.** Lay-flat is the width of the flattened tube. It is related to circumference, so it will usually be much larger than the outside diameter of the container it fits.
- **Ignoring band height.** Width answers "can the band get over the closure and shrink down?" Height answers "will it cover enough of the cap and neck to do the job?" Both have to be correct.
- **Buying at the edge of a fit range without testing.** Choose the next larger size when a measured diameter is near the top of a band's stated range, and test the product for your individual application. Samples are especially valuable when the closure has a flange or unusual contour.
- **Assuming a shrink band is a leak seal.** Do not treat tamper evidence and leak prevention as the same function. Several suppliers explicitly state that their shrink bands are not intended to prevent leakage. If leakage control matters, verify the liner, inner seal, induction seal, or other closure system separately.

## What Should You Check Before Ordering?

Once the geometry is clear, compare supplier specifications rather than choosing by nominal size alone. At minimum, confirm the exact **lay-flat width**, **cut height**, **material**, **thickness if stated**, **perforation style**, **published fit range**, and **recommended heating method**. Ask whether the supplier can provide samples in the candidate sizes.

Also check whether the band is appropriate for the actual container type. Some suppliers do not recommend their own bands for certain container/closure combinations, including plastic bottles, tapered cork closures, swing tops, and some straight-sided jars. That is supplier-specific, but it shows why compatibility should be verified rather than assumed.

For custom or bulk sourcing, also request the supplier's **tolerance**, **material specification**, **packing quantity**, **minimum order quantity (MOQ)**, **lead time**, and confirmation of the exact dimensions used in the quotation. Marketplace display prices and generic product titles are not enough to establish final commercial terms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a 28-400 neck mean I need a 28 mm shrink band?

No. A neck-finish code describes the closure interface, not the actual outside diameter of the assembled cap and container. Measure the widest outside diameter the band must pass over and use the supplier's fit data.

### How much larger should the unshrunk band be than the cap?

There is no single universal millimetre allowance. One published method adds 2 mm to half the measured circumference, while other suppliers publish direct minimum/maximum fit ranges. Follow the specification for the band you are buying.

### Can a shrink band be too large?

Yes. If the unshrunk tube is excessively large relative to the package, the available shrink may not produce the intended grip. A tamper-evident band also needs to bridge and grip the closure and container appropriately. Use the supplier's fit range and test the package.

### Do shrink bands stop bottles or jars from leaking?

Do not assume they do. Several suppliers state that their shrink bands are not intended to prevent leakage. Treat leak control as a separate packaging requirement and verify the closure or inner-seal system designed for that purpose.
