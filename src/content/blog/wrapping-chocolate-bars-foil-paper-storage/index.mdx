> **Quick answer.** The classic craft-bar wrap is thin aluminium foil, often waxed onto paper tissue, inside a paper sleeve or band. Industry sources say the foil sheet is a total barrier to light and moisture, while a former chocolatier writes that folded foil wraps are not fully vapour-tight and do not keep out insects. Both can be true, because the barrier is the sheet and the weak point is the fold. For storage, the sources the sources read give 12 to 18 degrees Celsius (54 to 64 Fahrenheit) with relative humidity at or below 70 percent from a chocolate maker's academy, 60 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit and under 65 percent from a retail brand, and 55 to 65 Fahrenheit and under 50 percent from a bulk-chocolate handling sheet. Pick a wrap, then test your bars against the storage range that matches your market.

## What chocolate needs to be protected from

The Callebaut-run Chocolate Academy gives the baseline: keep pure chocolate between 12 and 18 degrees Celsius, keep the room or case at or below 70 percent relative humidity, store in a draft-free place away from light and strong odours, and use packaging that is sturdy, odour-free and, where possible, airtight and light-blocking [1]. It says higher temperatures soften chocolate and dull its gloss, and that humidity above the limit lets products absorb moisture and lose crispness [1].

Two defects get named in almost every source. Fat bloom is a pale film that appears when chocolate has melted and re-set or been stored warm; Temperpack, a shipping-insulation supplier, says it typically follows exposure beyond roughly 80 to 90 degrees Fahrenheit, depending on the chocolate [5]. Sugar bloom is a gritty surface that develops when moisture reaches the chocolate, which Temperpack and Alter Eco both link to condensation after a cold-to-warm move [5][2]. A wrapper cannot undo either, but a good one slows the exposure.

## The two classic wrap structures

Rick Levine, a former chocolatier answering a question on Quora that HuffPost republished, describes two options for a wrapped bar. In an envelope wrap, the bar is covered in paper with neat folded ends, like hospital corners. In a band wrap, a foil laminate shows at the ends and a paper band goes around the middle [7]. Under either, he says, the foil is thin aluminium waxed onto a 15 pound paper tissue, which makes it easier to handle and reduces the metal needed while still resisting punctures [7].

Patent background supports that picture of a two-step process. One patent describes an inner wrapping of aluminium foil and an outer wrapping of paper, folded in two stations, with the outer wrap either complete or a slipover [9]. Another, for bar wrapping, describes a foil sheet with a sealing layer on the inside folded round the bar with a longitudinal fin seal and two end flaps, and notes that this leaves no room for printed messaging, which is why a second paper wrapper is customary [9]. Both are inventors' background statements, not recommendations for a small kitchen.

## Is foil a barrier or not? The sources disagree

The European aluminium foil association says aluminium foil or laminate offers the best protection for chocolate, with a total barrier to light, moisture and any penetration of aroma or flavour, and that foil and paper laminates add strength without losing the dead fold that suits bars [6]. That is an industry body promoting its material.

Levine says folded foil-and-tissue wraps are not fully vapour-tight and do not keep out insects, which matters for chocolate that can dry out or oxidise and for bars with nuts [7]. Mashed, summarising Hershey's reasons for dropping foil, says foil does not seal tightly and is not airtight or vapour-proof, though it still gives some protection against moisture and oxygen [8]. Hershey's own explanation, as reported there, is that plastic film can be sealed by heat or pressure, keeping air and contaminants out [8].

The two views fit together if you separate the sheet from the package. The metal sheet itself blocks light and gas; a hand-folded wrap has open edges, so air can still move through the folds. The reading here: a foil wrap buys light protection and a good extra layer, not an airtight seal. If your bars will sit for months, test them.

## Why craft makers still use foil

Mashed reports that smaller gourmet makers often keep the foil layer because they ship less product to fewer places, so shelf life matters less to them, and because sealing plastic the way Hershey does takes complex, high-powered machinery they generally cannot match [8]. It adds that foil looks more upscale and can be rewrapped after opening [8]. Levine adds that some buyers associate paper envelope wrapping with luxury, and gives industrial speeds for comparison: envelope-wrapping machines run roughly 100 to 200 bars a minute and a horizontal flow-wrapper about 800 packs a minute [7]. Those are factory numbers, not what you do on a bench.

## Storage numbers: how far the sources agree

|Source|Temperature|Humidity|
|---|---|---|
|Chocolate Academy (Callebaut) [1]|12 to 18 C (54 to 64 F)|At or below 70% RH|
|Alter Eco, retail brand [2]|60 to 70 F (15 to 21 C)|Below 65% RH if you can measure it|
|Hill Country Chocolate, retail brand [3]|60 to 70 F|50 to 55% RH; sugar bloom above 65%|
|Bulk chocolate handling sheet [4]|55 to 65 F|Under 50% RH|

Four different organisations, four ranges. The Fahrenheit-to-Celsius conversion for rows 2 to 4 is ours. None is a regulation.

The ranges overlap in the middle, around 15 to 18 degrees Celsius and 50 to 65 percent humidity, which is a defensible place to aim. The handling sheet is written for ingredient and finished-product storage by bulk customers [4], and the Academy is written for professional chocolatiers [1]. Neither is written for a market stall in summer.

Refrigeration is a trap. The Academy says it is generally not recommended because fridge humidity can raise product moisture, and advises letting packaged chocolate reach room temperature before opening to avoid condensation [1]. Alter Eco says to wrap tightly in foil or plastic first if you do refrigerate, and let the bar warm up before unwrapping [2]. Wrapping is the buffer between a cold bar and warm air.

## Hot climates and the freeze-then-seal idea

Hill Country Chocolate, a Texas maker, writes that it briefly chills or freezes bars before heat-sealing shrink film so the heat does not damage the temper, and that humidity above 65 percent triggers sugar bloom [3]. It also states that oxygen transmission should stay below 0.5 cc per square metre per 24 hours for optimal preservation and that metallised films and foil laminates perform well [3]. Those are one brand's blog claims; the sources reviewed show no independent test of the 0.5 figure. Note too that Hill Country uses sealed shrink film, which is a different structure from hand-folded foil.

For shipping in warm weather, Temperpack lists insulation options such as polystyrene foam, cotton fibre liners, starch-based foam, bubble wrap and paper liners, and says phase change materials can hold a temperature range for longer [5]. Our page on shipping candles in hot weather covers the wider logistics of heat; the same cautions apply to chocolate, with a lower threshold.

## Foil sheet size and the fold budget

The most common hand-wrapping mistake is a sheet that is too small. A bar wrapped in foil needs enough sheet to cover both faces, both long sides and the ends with overlap, so the sheet must exceed the bar's perimeter in one direction and its length in the other. Our arithmetic from a generic bar of 80 by 40 by 8 millimetres: the long wrap needs at least 2 x (40 + 8) = 96 millimetres around the bar, plus a 10 millimetre overlap, so about 106 millimetres across, and 80 millimetres plus two end folds of about 15 millimetres each, so about 110 millimetres along. A 110 by 110 millimetre sheet is a starting point. We have not tested this with real foil; measure your own bar and your own foil.

Dead-fold behaviour matters here. The foil association says foil and paper laminates keep the dead fold that suits sensitive products such as bars [6], which is why the folded wrap holds its shape. Thin unsupported foil creases easily and shows every fingerprint, so supplier samples of both a foil-on-tissue and a plain foil are worth requesting before you commit to a roll or a pack.

## Wrapping by hand: what we would do and test

This section is a suggestion, not from a source, and untested. A patent example describes chocolate that was firm enough for wrapping after cooling and a short rewarm, then held at 15 degrees Celsius for seven days, where gloss was rated good [10]. We cite it only to show that makers wrap bars after they are fully set and then let them rest in a cool store; it is not a method for you to copy.

- Let tempered bars set completely in a cool, dry room so there is no tackiness on the surface.

- Handle with clean, dry hands or food-safe gloves; fingerprints and moisture leave marks.

- Wrap the bar in foil with the fewest creases you can, fold the ends in the same direction each time, and press the edges flat.

- Slide on the paper band or fold the paper envelope over the foil, then add the label.

- Store the wrapped bars in a cool, dry box away from light and strong odours, as the Academy advises [1].

- Open one bar every week or two and compare it with a fresh bar: gloss, snap, smell, any pale film.

Label content, allergen statements and any claims on the wrapper are regulated food-labelling matters nothing here was research for this page; check your local rules before printing.

## A bench test for your own bars

Make a small batch of the same bar and wrap one set in foil plus paper, one in paper only, and keep one unwrapped as a control. Store each set at two conditions that match your market, for example a cool cupboard and a warm shelf. After two and four weeks, open a bar from each and compare gloss and smell. Then move a set from cool to warm, open it before it warms up and again after, and look for surface condensation or bloom. This test has not been run, and four weeks is a guess at a useful window. Write down wrap, condition, week and result; that table is your own shelf-life evidence.

## Questions to ask the supplier

- What is the foil thickness and is it laminated to paper, waxed or coated?

- Is the foil or laminate food-contact approved, and for which food types?

- What size sheet fits my bar with room for the folds, and is it pre-cut?

- Does the paper sleeve or band have a barrier or grease layer, and can it be printed?

- What is the minimum order, and can I buy a sample pack first?

## Common mistakes

- Treating a foil wrap as airtight. The sheet is a barrier; folded edges are not sealed [7][8].

- Refrigerating wrapped bars and unwrapping them cold. Condensation causes sugar bloom [1][2].

- Storing near strong smells. Chocolate takes up odours; packaging should be odour-free [1].

- Copying one source's humidity number. The four sources differ [1][2][3][4].

- Wrapping before the bars have fully set. Our caution, untested.

## FAQ

### What temperature should I store wrapped chocolate at?

Sources give about 12 to 18 degrees Celsius (54 to 64 F) [1] and 60 to 70 F [2][3]. Choose the overlap and test.

### Is foil better than plastic film for chocolate?

Foil blocks light and gas through the sheet [6] but folds are not sealed [7]. Plastic film can be heat sealed, which Hershey cites as a reason it switched [8], but that needs machinery.

### Why did my chocolate get a white film?

Fat bloom follows heat or temperature swings; sugar bloom follows moisture or condensation [5][2]. A wrapper slows both but cannot fix poor tempering.

### Can I put chocolate in the fridge?

Generally not recommended; if you must, wrap it and let it warm before opening [1][2].

### Do I need a humidity meter?

Every source above gives a humidity target, so a small hygrometer in the storage room is the cheapest way to know whether you are inside any of them [1][2][3][4]. Check it in summer and in the rainy season, when readings change the most.
