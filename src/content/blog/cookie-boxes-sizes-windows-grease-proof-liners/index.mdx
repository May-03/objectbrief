> **Quick answer.** Pick the box by what it must hold in three dimensions, not by a number of cookies on the label. Sellers' own pages show the same footprint holding very different counts: a reviewer fits two dozen decorated cookies in an 8 by 8 by 4 inch box, while the maker of a 4-slot insert for that same box says it holds four cupcakes. Height matters as much as footprint, because frosting and stacked cookies need clearance. For buttery cookies, line the box with glassine or greaseproof paper, and ask the supplier whether the paperboard carries a grease barrier, because a customer-review summary on one retailer's page notes that some boxes can show grease. In the United States, the FDA reported in February 2024 that PFAS-based grease-proofing agents are no longer being sold for paper food packaging, so a PFAS statement from your supplier is now a confirmation, not a differentiator.

## What sellers say fits in each size

Counts per box are the least reliable number on a listing, because they depend on cookie diameter and how snugly you pack. Still, the pages and reviews give a usable picture. Webstaurant's 8 by 4 by 4 inch box is described as holding two standard cupcakes [4], and its 8 by 8 by 4 inch box with a four-slot reversible insert is described as holding four standard cupcakes, although one reviewer on that page calls it the perfect size for six cupcakes [5]. A reviewer on the plain 8 by 8 by 4 inch box says it fits two dozen decorated cookies [6]. Another reviewer, on a 12 by 8 by 2 1/4 inch box, says it suits sets of a dozen sugar cookies and three dozen drop cookies [7]. A reviewer on a 12 by 12 by 2 3/4 inch box says it fits 9 inch cookie cakes [8].

Claws Custom Boxes, a box printer, offers a size guide: 4 by 4 by 2.5 inches for a single large cookie, a couple of standard cookies or four to five macarons, 5 by 5 by 2.5 for cake slices, 6 by 6 by 3 for frosted treats, and a slim 8.5 by 5 by 1.25 for decorated cookies or a row of macarons [9]. That is one seller's suggestion, not an industry standard.

Two dozen decorated cookies therefore means more than one layer or smaller cookies, and the reviewer does not say which. If you plan layers, you need dividers or interleaving paper between them, which is where the liner comes in.

## Height decides more than footprint

The footprint tells you how many cookies sit side by side. The height tells you whether frosting, sprinkles or stacked layers survive. Claws describes 6 by 6 by 3 inches as deep enough for frosted treats [9]. A reviewer on the 8 by 8 by 4 inch window cupcake box with an insert says it is tall enough for high frosting [5]. A slim 1.25 inch box, by contrast, is built for flat decorated cookies and has no room for anything piled [9].

For hand packing, measure your tallest finished item, add the height of any liner or insert, and leave a few millimetres above. Our rule of thumb is untested: if the lid presses on the product when closed, you will see smears in the first batch. Order a single box of the candidate size before a case.

## Windows: what they add and what they hide

A window shows the product and lets a customer judge freshness without opening the box. Retailers sell both auto-popup window boxes and plain ones in the same sizes [1]: Webstaurant lists an 8 by 8 by 4 inch window box described as auto-popup [5], and a 12 by 12 by 2 3/4 reviewer notes you can also turn a box inside out so the kraft shows [8]. Walmart marketplace listings describe 10 by 10 by 3 inch brown bakery boxes with a PVC window [10].

The window film is the part to ask about. Several pages call the box recyclable but none of the pages the sources read says whether the window film is separated from the paper or can go in the same recycling stream. Ask your supplier what the window is made of and whether it is cut out or has to be peeled. For a bakery selling at a market the practical effect is that the window can fog or smear if cookies are still warm, which is our observation about condensation, not a sourced fact.

## Grease: boxes, liners and what the papers are

Customer reviews on a bestselling Webstaurant box say some find them sturdy and professional, while a few note they can be flimsy or show grease [11]. One reviewer says earlier orders of the same box fit two dozen cookies perfectly but the last two orders were falling apart [6]. Those are individual reviews, not a defect rate, but they show why a liner is cheap insurance for butter-rich products. Walmart listings advertise cookie boxes as extra thick and oil resistant, a seller claim without a stated test [12].

Three liner papers get confused. Glassine is a smooth, glossy, translucent paper that resists grease and, to a degree, moisture, and is used for baked goods, candy wrappers and butter wrapping, according to the packaging maker Refine Packaging [13]. A paper trade supplier describes glassine as achieving oil and grease resistance through dense fibre structure rather than chemical coatings, in a typical 28 to 70 gsm range, and says it offers stronger resistance for oily products such as chocolate or butter than ordinary greaseproof [14]. Limepack describes greaseproof paper as oil resistant, non-stick and suited to wrapping food, and parchment as a separate cellulose paper that tears easily and does not seal well, so it does not recommend parchment for packaging [15].

Limepack's page gives conflicting heat limits for greaseproof paper, one figure of 215 to 230 degrees Celsius, another of 220 and a third saying not above 190 [15]. That inconsistency is a reason not to use a trade blog for oven guidance. For box lining at room temperature the heat figure does not matter; the grease resistance does.

The practical choice for a small bakery is therefore two-step: ask for a food-contact glassine or greaseproof sheet cut to the box floor or sized to wrap each cookie, and test it with your own dough. Nothing here was test any liner; the bench test below shows how.

## PFAS: what the FDA has said

PFAS were long used as grease-proofing agents on paper and paperboard packaging. In February 2024 the FDA announced that substances containing PFAS were no longer being sold into the U.S. market for use as grease-proofing agents on paper food packaging, following voluntary phase-out commitments by manufacturers and earlier FDA actions [16]. In January 2025 the FDA issued a notice that 35 related food contact notifications were no longer effective because manufacturers had ceased production, supply or use [17].

Webstaurant labels some of its paper bags "No PFAS Added" for California [2]. The reading here: for US-made and US-sold paperboard, the FDA's statement covers the sale of grease-proofing agents containing PFAS for food-contact use, so a supplier saying "no PFAS added" is unlikely to differ from the market baseline. For imported boxes, ask for a written statement from the manufacturer, because the sources reviewed show nothing that says every import is covered. We are not lawyers, and state rules differ, so check your own state.

## Assembly, stacking and storage

Boxes arrive flat, and the assembly style affects your packing speed. Webstaurant's 8 by 4 by 4 inch box has a one-piece design with lock corners and a lid that tucks back into place [4], and its auto-popup window boxes are described as a time-saving alternative to traditional fold-and-lock boxes [19]. A reviewer on one of these boxes calls them easy to assemble [3]. For a market stall packing fifty boxes before opening, the difference between a pop-up and a fold-and-lock design is minutes, which you can time yourself during the bench test.

Flat boxes also take little storage space, which is an argument for ordering the next size up only when you need it rather than a year's supply. A case of 200 is a lot of cardboard for a bakery that sells forty boxes a week, so ask whether the supplier sells in packs of ten, as several of these listings do [1][4]. Nothing here was test stacking strength; the test above includes two stacked boxes for that reason.

## A bench test before you order a case

- Buy one box each of two sizes, plus a few sheets of glassine and greaseproof paper.

- Pack the same cookies in each, with and without a liner, and close the lids.

- Leave them overnight at room temperature, then check the box floor and lid for dark patches. Do this with your butteriest recipe.

- Shake and tilt each box gently, then carry two stacked boxes across a room to see if lids pop open or cookies slide.

- Check at the cookie edges for smears from the lid. If any appear, move to the next height.

- Write down the box, size, liner, cookie count and result. That table becomes your packing spec.

One overnight grease test is a screening step and not a shelf-life study; it has not been run and nobody should treat it as a validated method.

## Questions to ask the supplier

- What are the interior dimensions, not just the outside size?

- Does the paperboard have a grease barrier or a coating, and is it PFAS-free according to a written statement?

- What is the window film made of, and can the box be recycled with it?

- Can I get a sample of each size before a case, and what is the box board weight?

- Does the box ship flat, and how many seconds does assembly take by hand?

## Common mistakes

- Buying by cookie count. The same box is described with different counts by different people [5][6].

- Ignoring height. A flat box crushes frosting [9].

- Skipping a liner for butter-rich cookies. Reviews mention grease showing on boxes [11].

- Taking a "oil resistant" claim at face value. No test is stated [12].

- Using parchment as a wrapper. One supplier says it tears easily and does not seal well [15].

## FAQ

### What size box holds a dozen cookies?

Sellers and reviewers give 12 by 8 by 2 1/4 inches for a dozen sugar cookies [7] and 8 by 8 by 4 inches for two dozen decorated cookies [6]. Your cookie diameter decides.

### Do I need a liner?

For dry cookies, probably not for same-day sale; for butter-rich cookies it is cheap protection. Reviews mention grease showing on some boxes [11].

### Is glassine the same as parchment?

No. Glassine is a glossy grease-resistant paper [13]; parchment is a separate cellulose paper that one supplier says tears easily [15].

### Are US food-contact papers still treated with PFAS?

The FDA reported in 2024 that PFAS-based grease-proofing agents are no longer sold for this use in the US [16]. Ask for a written supplier statement on imports.
