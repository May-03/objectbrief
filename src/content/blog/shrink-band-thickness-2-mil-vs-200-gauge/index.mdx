> **Quick answer.** A 2 mil shrink band is 0.002 inch thick, which equals 0.0508 mm or 50.8 micrometers. In the U.S. flexible-film trade, 200 gauge is commonly used as another way to describe the same nominal thickness: 100 gauge = 1 mil, so 200 gauge = 2 mil. That gauge relationship is an industry convention rather than an SI unit, so when a supplier uses "gauge," confirm what its specification means instead of assuming the label is universal.

The more important buying point is what thickness does not tell you. A 2 mil band is not automatically a 50% or 60% shrink film, and a 200-gauge film is not automatically "stronger" or "better" than every thinner option. Thickness, unrestrained thermal shrinkage, shrink tension, material, orientation, perforation design, and final package fit are separate variables. Normalize the units first, then compare the rest of the specification on its own terms.

## What Does "2 Mil" Mean on a Shrink Band?

The first trap is the word mil. In packaging, a mil is one-thousandth of an inch. It is not a millimeter and it is not a milliliter. NIST lists 1 mil as exactly 0.001 inch, 0.0254 mm, or 25.4 micrometers.

That makes the common conversions straightforward. Two mil is 0.002 inch, 0.0508 mm, or 50.8 micrometers. A supplier quoting 50 micrometers is therefore very close to 2 mil, but it is not mathematically identical: 50 micrometers is about 1.97 mil. Likewise, 40 micrometers is about 1.57 mil and 60 micrometers is about 2.36 mil.

For procurement, keep the original supplier unit in the RFQ and add one normalized unit next to it. That prevents a buyer from silently rounding 40 micrometers up to "2 mil" or treating 0.05 mm and 0.05 mil as the same thing.

## Is 200 Gauge the Same as 2 Mil?

You will see both descriptions in the current market. Uline lists many stock PVC shrink bands as 200 gauge, while SKS Bottle & Packaging describes multiple PVC bands as 2 mil thick. Those pages illustrate why buyers encounter two vocabularies for similar packaging components.

Under the common U.S. film convention, 100 gauge equals 1 mil. On that basis, 200 gauge equals 2 mil and therefore 50.8 micrometers. The conversion is useful for comparing quotes, but "gauge" should still be treated as a trade convention that needs supplier confirmation. Unlike mil, millimeter, and micrometer, film gauge is not the thickness unit defined in the NIST conversion table.

A safe purchase order does not stop at "200 gauge." Write the nominal thickness in a physical unit as well, for example: nominal thickness 2 mil (50.8 µm), supplier to confirm tolerance and test method. That removes most of the ambiguity before samples arrive.

## Why Thickness Is Not the Same as Shrink Rate

Thickness measures how thick the film is. Shrink rate describes how much the oriented film changes dimension under defined heating conditions. They are not the same property.

ASTM treats them separately. D6988 is a guide for determining plastic-film thickness. D2732 addresses unrestrained linear thermal shrinkage at specified specimen temperatures. D2838 addresses shrink tension and orientation release stress. The existence of separate methods is a useful procurement lesson: a thickness number cannot substitute for the film's shrink curve, shrink percentage at a stated temperature, or the force it can exert while recovering.

This matters when a supplier page places thickness and a "shrink ratio" beside each other. A buyer may be tempted to think that a thicker film shrinks more. That conclusion does not follow. ASTM D2732 notes that the magnitude of shrinkage varies with film temperature and that the temperature at which shrinkage occurs is related to manufacturing technique and may also relate to a transition in the base resin. Orientation and process history matter.

For a neck band, therefore, specify thickness because it affects the physical component you are buying, but verify shrink behavior separately for the exact material and package.

## Does a Thicker Shrink Band Mean Better Performance?

Not by itself. A thicker band may feel stiffer or more substantial, but "thicker = better tamper evidence" is too simple for a purchase specification. A tamper-evident band has to fit the closure correctly, shrink without uncontrolled wrinkles or splits, bridge the area that must show opening, and tear in the intended way when perforated.

Material and orientation also change performance. ASTM D2838 notes that orientation influences properties such as tensile strength, stiffness, tear resistance, and impact strength. That means two films at the same nominal thickness can behave differently if the resin, orientation, converting, perforation, or heating process differs.

The reverse is also important: a thinner film should not automatically be rejected as weak. If the supplier designed that film for the required shrink geometry and the applied samples pass your opening, tear, transport, and appearance checks, the nominal number may be entirely appropriate.

Use thickness as one controlled field, not as a quality score. For small brands, applied-package testing usually gives more decision value than arguing over a few micrometers in isolation.

## 2 Mil PVC vs 50 µm PETG: What Can You Actually Compare?

A material change is a separate decision from a thickness change. A PVC-versus-PETG comparison is its own material choice; this guide only explains how to normalize thickness language.

If one quote says 2 mil PVC and another says 50 µm PETG, the thicknesses are nearly equivalent numerically, but the offers are not equivalent products. The resin system, orientation, shrink window, shrink force, printing/converting behavior, and recycling context can differ. Do not conclude that the PETG quote is a direct substitute just because the micrometer number is close.

Likewise, a supplier switch from 40 µm to 50.8 µm changes more than a spreadsheet cell. Run the new band on the actual closure with the intended heat source. Check how quickly it begins to recover, whether it bridges the cap-to-neck transition cleanly, whether the perforation starts prematurely, and whether the finished band can be removed as intended.

Thickness normalization tells you whether the numbers are comparable. It does not prove interchangeability.

## How to Read Supplier Thickness Fields Without Guessing

Supplier listings can create three different comparison problems. First, one supplier gives thickness in mil while another gives micrometers. Second, a marketplace field can be mislabeled or populated with a dimension that is not thickness. Third, the page may provide a wide customizable range rather than the actual production specification you will receive.

Normalize the units, but preserve the evidence status. If a listing says "25–70 micron," record that as a stated customization range. Do not silently convert it into "the product is 50 microns." If a page says "0.03–0.12 mm," record the same range as 30–120 µm and ask which nominal thickness applies to the quoted band size, material, print, and MOQ.

If the field itself looks wrong—for example, a value that resembles width × height rather than film thickness—mark it as a listing-data conflict and request a controlled specification from the supplier. Marketplace attributes are discovery data, not a substitute for a signed order specification.

## What Should You Put on the RFQ or Purchase Specification?

For a repeat purchase, "PVC shrink band, custom size" is not a complete specification. At minimum, lock the fields that affect what arrives and how it behaves on your line.

Use one thickness unit as the contractual primary unit and place any conversion in parentheses. If the supplier manufactures in micrometers, let micrometers be the controlled value rather than forcing an exact mil number that exists only because of conversion. Ask for nominal thickness and tolerance if thickness variation matters to application consistency.

Then pair thickness with the other independent fields: material or supplier grade, lay-flat width, cut height, perforation location, print requirement, shrink behavior under a defined condition where relevant, sample approval, and packaging format. If the supplier quotes a shrink percentage, ask what direction, temperature, dwell/conditioning method, and test basis the number represents.

Finally, keep a retained or photographed approved sample. A number on a PO is useful; a known-good band shrunk on the actual bottle or jar is even more useful when a later shipment feels or behaves different.

| RFQ field | What to request | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material / grade | PVC, PETG, or exact supplier film grade | Thickness alone does not define shrink behavior |
| Nominal thickness | One controlled unit, e.g. 50 µm (1.97 mil) | Creates a comparable physical specification |
| Thickness tolerance | Supplier-stated tolerance if process-sensitive | Controls lot-to-lot variation |
| Lay-flat + cut height | Finished pre-shrink dimensions | Separates size from thickness |
| Perforation | Position / count / tear direction | Changes opening and failure behavior |
| Shrink behavior | Defined direction, temperature and method where quoted | Prevents "shrink %" from becoming an undefined marketing number |
| Sample approval | Applied sample on real bottle/jar and closure | Tests the complete package rather than one isolated field |
| Change control | Notify buyer before material/thickness/process change | Protects repeatability on reorders |

## A Simple Quote-Normalization Workflow

The practical workflow is simple. Start by copying the supplier's original thickness claim exactly. Convert it into a common comparison unit, preferably micrometers for cross-border quotes. Flag whether the value is nominal, a range, or unclear. Then compare material and shrink properties separately instead of merging them into one "film quality" judgment.

For example, Quote A: PVC, 2 mil. Normalize to 50.8 µm. Quote B: PVC, 40 µm. Normalize to 1.57 mil. Quote C: PETG, 50 µm. Normalize to 1.97 mil, but mark material as different and require a separate substitution test. If Quote C also says "70% shrink," do not use that percentage to rank it against A or B until the test direction and temperature are defined.

This method keeps the unit conversion mechanical and the engineering decision evidence-based. It also makes RFQs easier to audit because every comparison can be traced back to the supplier's original field.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is 2 mil the same as 0.05 mm?

Nearly, but not exactly. Two mil is exactly 0.0508 mm. A nominal 0.05 mm film is 50 µm, which is about 1.97 mil.

### Is 200 gauge the same as 2 mil?

Under the common U.S. flexible-film convention, yes: 100 gauge = 1 mil, so 200 gauge = 2 mil. Confirm the supplier's use of "gauge" because it is an industry convention rather than an SI thickness unit.

### How many microns is 2 mil?

50.8 micrometers.

### Does a thicker shrink band need more heat?

Do not infer a heat setting from thickness alone. Material, orientation, film construction, equipment, container geometry, and supplier processing guidance all matter. Validate the exact band with the intended heat source.

### Does 50 µm mean the film has a 50% shrink rate?

No. Micrometers measure thickness. Percent shrink measures dimensional change under defined conditions. They are different specifications.

### Should I request a thickness tolerance?

If thickness consistency affects application, print, perforation, or repeatability, yes. Ask the supplier to state the nominal thickness, tolerance, and the measurement basis used for the supplied film.
